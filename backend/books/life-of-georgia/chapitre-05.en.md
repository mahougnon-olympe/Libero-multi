# Chapter 5: The Wassa Forest

On the first night, they slept out under the stars, at the edge of the forest. Georgia kept watch while Jordan slept. She watched this prince who had done her so much harm and wondered whether she was right to help him. But deep down, a small voice told her that no one is born evil, and that Jordan was, above all, the victim of the sorcerer Kadel.

On the morning of the second day, they plunged into the forest. The Wassa trees were so tall and so dense that the sunlight barely reached the ground. Strange sounds echoed from everywhere: cracklings, hissings, distant laughter that was nothing human.

"Stay close to me," said Georgia.

"I'm the prince, I'm the one who should protect you," Jordan grumbled.

"A prince with no powers and no weapon? Don't make me laugh," Georgia replied.

It was at that precise moment that Kadel's first trap closed on them. The ground began to shake, and from the earth rose three creatures made of mud and roots: the Wassaians, corrupted guardians of the forest. Their eyes glowed with a green light, the same light as the sorcerer's.

Georgia hurled a fireball at the first one, which collapsed into ashes. But the other two were already on Jordan. One of them grabbed him by the leg and dragged him toward a chasm that had opened between the trees.

"Georgia!!!" screamed the prince.

Georgia hesitated for a second. She could let him fall, literally. After everything he had done to her, no one would have blamed her. But she thought back to the witch's words: the lake lets itself be found only by those with a pure heart. If she abandoned Jordan now, her own heart would be worth no more than his.

She stretched out both hands, and a torrent of flames swept away the two creatures. Jordan hung at the edge of the chasm, clinging to a root. Georgia hauled him up with all her strength.

"You... you saved me," Jordan breathed, still trembling.

"Don't thank me. Just become someone worth saving," Georgia replied.

Those words struck Jordan harder than any fireball.

In the days that followed, something changed. Jordan gathered wood for the fire without being asked. He shared his food. One evening, as a violent rain came down on the forest, he even gave his jacket to Georgia and slept in the cold. Kadel's spell was still within him, but it seemed a light was beginning to break through the darkness of his heart.

On the fifth day, they finally reached the edge of the forest. Before them rose the three twin mountains, immense, their peaks lost in the clouds.

But what they did not know was that Kadel had been watching them from the start through the eyes of his creatures. And what he had just seen displeased him greatly: his spell was weakening. The sorcerer rose from his throne of bones and took down his cloak.

"Since my creatures are not enough," he said in an icy voice, "I will deal with them myself."
