# Chapter 12: What We Become

The return took seven days, like the journey out. But they were seven very different days.

In Toko, they were welcomed as heroes. The woman with the round face held her recovered son against her and did not let go of him all evening. Dah Gbeto, the old chief, listened to the account of the battle in silence, then laid his hand on Jordan's shoulder:

"You have a promise to keep, Prince."

"I haven't forgotten."

And he did not forget.

Back in Black-Fish, Jordan summoned the kingdom's council, his aunt Mia, and the judges. Before them all, he handed Kadel over to justice. The trial lasted weeks: the people of Toko came to testify one by one, and for the first time, the whole kingdom heard the names of the Forgotten. Kadel, stripped of his magic, was condemned to spend the rest of his days in the kingdom's prison, and to rebuild with his own hands, stone by stone, everything he had destroyed.

But Jordan did not stop there. Before the full council, he spoke one last time:

"Kadel is not the only one who must answer for his deeds. Under the sway of his spell, but with my own hands, I committed a crime against an innocent. The spell explains what I became. It does not excuse what I did."

A stunned murmur ran through the hall. Aunt Mia rose, pale: "Jordan, be quiet, you are the prince..."

"That is precisely why I cannot be quiet, Aunt Mia. A kingdom where the prince is above the law is a kingdom that will make more Kadels."

Jordan renounced the throne for ten years. He spent the first year at the villagers' boarding school, the very one his father had once threatened him with, no longer as a punishment to flee but as a school one chooses. The following years he spent on the kingdom's roads, rebuilding old Codjo's fields, building a school in Toko, repairing, wherever it was possible, whatever could be repaired.

Georgia, for her part, finally went home to Aboula.

Her parents, who had been mourning her for months, saw her appear one morning at the end of the path, and the whole village heard her mother's cry of joy. She told them everything: Black-Fish, the castle, the Wassa forest, the three mountains, the lake. Her father listened all the way through, then simply declared:

"I told you never to trust a man's beauty. I should have added: but always trust my daughter's strength."

The family of supers finally welcomed its youngest among their own. Georgia, whose powers had fully awakened, became the protector of the region: never again would a sorcerer, a bandit, or a prince harm the village girls with impunity. The years passed, and her name traveled far beyond Aboula. The children who crossed her path on the roads recognized her by her brown, silky hair, which, they said, glowed in the dark when she was angry.

As for Jordan and Georgia, life made their paths cross again, now and then. A school building site here, a stray creature there. Each time, they greeted each other, exchanged a bit of news, sometimes a meal.

One evening, years later, as they watched the sun set over the fairy lake, to which they had returned on a pilgrimage, Jordan asked softly:

"Did you ever forgive me?"

Georgia gazed for a long while at the little lights dancing on the water.

"I did better than that," she said at last. "I lived. I was happy. And you became someone the village girl would no longer be afraid to meet on her road. That's all I ever asked of time."

The fairies danced on the silver lake.

And somewhere in the world, a young girl was leaving her village to go and meet her life, wise, clever, and stronger than she yet knew.
