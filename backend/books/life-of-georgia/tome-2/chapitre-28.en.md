# Chapter 28: The Words That Had to Be Heard

The silent army withdrew at sunset, as suddenly as it had come, leaving Black-Fish standing, exhausted, and full of prisoners who wept.

For that was the strange spoils of this battle: three hundred captives, and Naya going from one to the next with the ivory dagger, cutting the threads one by one. Each time, the same miracle: the empty gaze filling up again, the cry, the tears, and people asking how long it had been, and weeping even harder on learning of the lost months. By dawn, Naya could barely stand, but three hundred people had come home to themselves.

"He will not come back right away," Jordan said at the war council. "He lost three hundred threads tonight, and he has learned that we can cut. He will reinforce his web, change his method. We have gained time, not the war. And while we hold a siege, the lake is draining, and Georgia is feeding it all."

That night, Naya could not find Jordan in the palace. She finally learned from Sergeant Tovi that the king had left alone, on horseback, with two guards ordered to keep their distance.

"Left for where?"

"The north," said Tovi. "He said he had a message to deliver. He said it could not wait any longer."

Jordan rode four days. He left his guards at the edge of the last wood, went on alone, and reached the walls of the fortress by night, through the eastern ravine, the one from Georgia's notebook. He knew he was being watched. He knew Sètondji was letting him approach out of curiosity, or respect, or to listen. It did not matter. He had not come to fight.

There was no window onto the great hall, but he did not need one. He sat on a stone in the ravine, facing the wall behind which the cocoon turned, and he spoke. In a low voice at first, then normally, the way one speaks to someone in the same room.

"It is me. It is Jordan. I do not know if you can hear me, in there. They say you are dreaming. So perhaps my voice can enter your dream, the way rain enters sleep."

He was quiet a moment, searching for where to begin after fifteen years.

"All these years, we crossed paths on the roads. You protecting, me repairing. We greeted each other. You asked me for news of the schools, I asked you for news of Aboula, and we each went our own way, polite as two strangers. Fifteen years of politeness, Georgia. Fifteen years of telling myself: next time, I will tell her. And every time, I found myself a good reason. She deserves better than a man with my past. She is a legend, I am a penance. You see: even my silences were pride."

The wind passed through the ravine. Somewhere on the wall, a marked sentinel watched him without moving, and Jordan knew Sètondji was listening through those eyes. He went on anyway. Perhaps that was the courage he had left.

"One day, long ago, you walked beside me in silence while I emptied my heart, and that silence saved my life more surely than the fairy lake. So tonight I have come to return the favour, the other way round. You spend your life carrying others, Georgia. You believe that if you let go, the world falls. And that intelligent monster has made your greatest strength your prison: the more you burn, the stronger his walls hold."

He rose and laid his hand on the cold rock of the ravine.

"So listen to me well, wherever you are in your dream. The world will not fall. There is a girl of sixteen, with an incredible eye and your bad temper, who is teaching us all how to cut threads. You are no longer alone in carrying. You never were. You are going to have to believe me on this, just once in your mule-headed life."

His voice cracked a little on the last words. He caught his breath.

"Hold on. We are coming for you. And when you come out of there, I will say to your face what I have just said to a wall."

He mounted his horse and rode back into the night.

At the heart of the cocoon, in a beautiful dream designed by a methodical man, a sleeping woman frowned, like someone who hears the rain.
