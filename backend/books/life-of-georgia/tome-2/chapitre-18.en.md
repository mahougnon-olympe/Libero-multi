# Chapter 18: The Weary Prince

Black-Fish was the largest city Naya had ever seen. There were more houses in a single street than in all of Kpassè, and the royal palace, at the top of the hill, seemed to carry the sky on its rooftops.

It took Naya three days to obtain an audience, and even then she did not obtain it honestly. The guards at the first gate had sent her away laughing. Those at the second had threatened her with the whip. So, on the third day, she planted herself in front of the captain of the guard and told him, very calmly:

"Yesterday, you swore to the king that the eastern soldiers' pay had been settled. That was a lie; I saw it come out of your mouth. Let me in, or I will say it louder."

The captain turned grey as ash, and Naya went in.

She was led into a high hall where King Jordan received the petitions of his people. Naya barely recognised him. In the tales of Kpassè, Jordan was a young, cruel prince who had become good. The man seated on the throne had grey hair, a back beginning to stoop, and above all eyes of a deep weariness, the weariness of those who are repaying a debt that never ends.

"Speak, little one," he said in a gentle voice. "But be quick; there are thirty people behind you."

"My name is Naya, Majesty. I have come from the east to show you something that no one but me can see."

She unfolded the piece of wrap.

She had imagined a hundred reactions. Anger, laughter, the order to throw her out. She had not imagined silence. Jordan stared at the drawing without moving, without breathing, and his face aged before her eyes, as if every charcoal stroke cost him a year.

"Have everyone leave," he said at last.

When the hall was empty, he came down from his throne and sat on the steps, right next to Naya, like an ordinary man.

"Where did you see this?"

"On the wrist of a farmer, in Sèmè. He no longer speaks, he works like a machine, and he sends carts of provisions toward the west. A thread of green light ties him to something very far away. No one sees the mark. I see magic, Majesty. I have always seen it."

"And why come and show it to me?"

"Because an old witch of the hills told me you are the only man in the kingdom to have carried this sign in his flesh."

Jordan closed his eyes. A long moment passed. Then, slowly, he rolled up the sleeve of his left arm.

Naya held back a cry. On the king's wrist there was nothing visible to ordinary eyes, only the aged skin of an ageing man. But she could see: a scar of light, green and pale, extinguished for fifteen years, which had begun to glow faintly again, like an ember being blown on from very far away.

"For weeks it has burned me at night," Jordan said in a low voice. "I told myself it was rheumatism. That it was memory. That it was anything except what it was. I have spent fifteen years repairing what I helped destroy, little one, and I think I hoped I had earned the right never to hear of this mark again."

He pulled his sleeve back down and looked Naya full in the face.

"Kadel has no magic left. The fairies took it from him before my very eyes. If somewhere there is a man tracing this sign again, then someone has learned his art. And there is only one place in the world where that art is still written."

He rose, and for the first time since the audience began, Naya saw something in his eyes other than weariness. Something harder, older. What remained of the prince who had once walked alone toward a lake guarded by a monster.

"Prepare to travel, Naya of the east. Tomorrow, we go to see the only man capable of explaining to us what is happening."

"Who, Majesty?"

"My old master. Kadel."
