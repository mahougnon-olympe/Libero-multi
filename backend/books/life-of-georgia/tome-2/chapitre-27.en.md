# Chapter 27: The Army of the Marked

Sètondji did not wait for Jordan's answer. He sent for it himself.

Three weeks after their return to Black-Fish, at dawn, the city's bells began to ring all at once. Naya ran up onto the ramparts with the king, and what she saw took her breath away.

They came from the north, the east and the west, by every road at once. Thousands of people. Not soldiers: people. Farmers with their hoes, blacksmiths with their hammers, women with pestles, woodcutters with their axes, marching in step, in total silence, without banner and without drum. An army that does not shout is the most frightening thing in the world, and above every head, to Naya's eye, quivered a green thread stretched toward the north.

A single man advanced within earshot of the ramparts, a marked man with an empty gaze, and his mouth opened to let out a voice that was not his own: the calm, young voice of Sètondji.

"Majesty. My offer remains open. Open the gates, yield me the regency, and not a drop of blood will flow. You have until noon."

On the ramparts, the general of Black-Fish gave the natural order, the order of every general in the world: archers in position. And that is when Jordan gave the order that would make this day impossible.

"No one shoots to kill. No one. These people are my subjects. That is the farmer of Sèmè, that is the miller of Danko, those are mothers and sons who have been stolen from themselves. If we massacre them to save ourselves, Sètondji will be right down the line: we are worth no more than the negligence he denounces. We push back, we knock out, we capture. We do not kill."

"Majesty," said the general in a colourless voice, "one does not win a battle with sticks against axes."

"Then we will not win a battle," Jordan replied. "We will hold a siege."

Noon rang. The silent army advanced.

What followed, the chronicles of Black-Fish would later call the battle of the bound hands. The king's soldiers fought with shields, fishing nets, poles and buckets of water, against opponents who knew neither fear nor pain. They pushed away the ladders instead of severing the hands that held them. They poured soapy water down the slopes to make the attackers slip. The women of the city threw sandbags; the fishermen cast their nets from the towers. And everywhere, running along the ramparts, a sixteen-year-old girl shouted what no one else could see.

"West gate, there are two hundred of them, but only forty are marked; the others are looters taking advantage; those ones, hit hard! The big blacksmith on the ladder, sergeant: his thread is almost out, he is fighting it from inside, take him alive!"

For that was Naya's discovery, in the midst of the chaos: the threads were not all alike. Some quivered, thick and bright. Others weakened, trembled, as if the person at the end were fighting their own mark. People's will was fighting, down there. The mark held the bodies, but the hearts bucked like hobbled horses, and the more Sètondji pulled on thousands of threads at once, the thinner each thread became.

It was on seeing this that Naya finally understood the ivory dagger.

She raced down the rampart stairs, ran to the east gate where the soldiers had just captured the big blacksmith, bound and struggling in silence. She drew the white blade, and before the eyes of ten soldiers who saw only a gesture in empty air, she cut the green thread an inch above the man's head.

The thread snapped like a harp string. The blacksmith arched, let out the first cry of the whole battle, an immense cry, human, magnificent, and collapsed sobbing into the soldiers' arms.

"Where am I?" he stammered. "Where am I? I was in the dark, I was striking my hammer in the dark, it never ended..."

Naya raised her head toward the ramparts, toward the thousands of threads streaking the sky, and for the first time since Kpassè, her extra eye showed her not what threatened the world, but how to save it.

One thread at a time.
