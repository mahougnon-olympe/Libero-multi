# Chapter 19: The Stone Prison

Kadel's prison was not a dungeon. It was a village.

Fifteen years earlier, after the trial, the kingdom's tribunal had sentenced the fallen sorcerer to rebuild with his own hands everything he had destroyed. He had been settled in the valley of the Three Villages, the very one he had once burned, under the guard of four soldiers. Since then, stone after stone, wall after wall, an old man had been rebuilding.

When Jordan and Naya arrived, escorted by a dozen riders, they found him on top of a low wall, setting a stone with the care of a jeweller. Naya had expected a monster. She saw a thin old man, his hands ruined by mortar, his white hair cropped short, who turned toward them without surprise.

"Jordan," he said simply. "Twenty-three walls this year. I am improving."

"Kadel," the king replied. "We need to talk."

The old man climbed down from the wall, wiped his hands, and looked at Naya. She held his gaze, and her extra eye confirmed what the witch had said: there was nothing left in him. Not a thread, not a glimmer, not the slightest trace of magic. This man was as empty as an upturned calabash.

Jordan held out the piece of wrap.

Kadel looked at the drawing. His hands began to tremble, but not like a guilty man's. Like those of a father being told of a tragedy.

"Where?" he asked in a flat voice.

"In the east," said Naya. "And according to a witch, in the north too, near the Wassa forest, a year ago."

"It is my tracing," Kadel murmured. "The anchor loop, the knot of will, it is all there. But it is finer than mine. Cleaner. Whoever did this corrected my flaws."

He sat down heavily on a stone, and for a long moment he looked out at the valley he was rebuilding.

"Jordan, there is something I must tell you, and you are going to hate me again. For six or seven years, a young man used to come and see me. Twice a year, sometimes three times. He introduced himself as a student from the school of Toko; he said he was writing the history of the war of the lake so that its mistakes would never be repeated. He was brilliant, respectful, patient. He asked the right questions."

"And you answered him," Jordan said in a colourless voice.

"I told him everything. My birth without power, the bargain with the spirit of the depths, the making of the marks, the battle, my defeat. I thought I was doing useful work, you understand? I thought that telling of evil was disarming it. He noted everything in a grey notebook. He nodded in the right places. He said: what a tragedy, Master Kadel, how you must have suffered."

The old man raised toward Jordan eyes full of a pain Naya would never forget.

"He was not taking notes to understand my mistakes, Jordan. He was taking notes to repeat them without repeating them wrong."

"His name," said Jordan.

"He called himself Sètondji. A child of the Village of the Forgotten, he said, saved by your school. The last time he came was a little over a year ago. He asked me a single question, a question no one had ever asked me: Master, if you had been able to drain magic instead of giving it, would you have lost?"

Naya felt the cold climb up her spine.

"And what did you answer?" she asked.

"The truth," said Kadel. "That no. That if I had known how to take instead of give, no one in the world could have stopped me, not even the fairies."

Silence fell over the valley of the Three Villages. On top of the wall, a badly set stone tipped over and shattered on the ground.

"There is something else," Kadel added very softly. "A year ago, a few weeks after his last visit, a woman passed along the road, over there, at the end of the valley. She did not stop. She was walking north, fast, like someone following a trail. Even from afar, even after fifteen years, there is no mistaking that silhouette."

He looked at Jordan.

"She was going toward him, Jordan. And she did not know what he had learned to take."
