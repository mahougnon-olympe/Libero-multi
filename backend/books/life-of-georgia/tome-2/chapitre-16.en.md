# Chapter 16: Aboula Without Georgia

It took Naya nine more days to reach Aboula, and when she finally arrived, legs heavy and sandals worn through, she understood at once that something was wrong.

In the tales, Aboula was the protector's village, a joyful place where people slept soundly because the woman with hair of fire watched over them. But the village Naya discovered was silent, and the faces there were shut like doors on a stormy day.

"Excuse me," she asked a young man mending a net, "I am looking for Georgia's house."

The young man looked up, and his gaze hardened.

"Another one," he said. "Every month they come: the curious, the pilgrims, the sick who want to be touched by the legend. Go home, little one. Georgia is gone."

"Gone? But... where is she?"

"If anyone knew," the young man replied, going back to his net, "there would not be so much sadness here."

Naya found the house all the same, the largest in the village, the one built for the family of supers the tales spoke of. She knocked, her heart tight, and an old man came to open. He had the broad shoulders of a wrestler and the tired eyes of a father.

"My name is Naya," she said, bowing. "I come from Kpassè, in the east, fourteen days' walk from here. My grandmother sent me to Georgia because... because I see things no one else sees."

The old man looked at her for a long time. Then he sighed and stepped aside from the door.

"Come in. I am her father."

Inside, around a dish no one really touched, Naya learned the truth that had not yet reached the tales of the east: Georgia had been missing for a year. One morning, she had taken her bag, said she was going to check something in the north, that she would be back before the rainy season. The rains had come twice. Georgia, never.

"Some say she is dead," murmured a woman with hands of steel, one of the sisters. "Others say she left because she was tired of carrying us all. Me, I say we would have felt her die. A flame like that does not go out without making smoke."

"Did she say what she was going to check in the north?" asked Naya.

"No. Georgia never said anything. She protected people even from her own worries."

Then Naya, her fingers trembling, took from her bag the piece of wrap on which she had drawn the green mark of the farmer of Sèmè, and she laid it in the middle of the dish.

"Has anyone ever seen this sign?"

The silence that fell in the room was so thick you could hear the goats outside. Georgia's father had risen, very pale. The sister with hands of steel had brought her hand to her mouth.

"Where did you see this?" the father asked in a colourless voice.

"On a man's wrist, in Sèmè, four days from here. A man who no longer speaks and works like a machine. But no one except me could see the mark."

"Little one," the old man said slowly, "this sign nearly destroyed the kingdom fifteen years ago. It is Kadel's mark. And Kadel has been rotting in a Black-Fish prison all this time, without a drop of magic."

That night, Naya was given a small room. Passing down the corridor, she stopped dead before a closed door. It was Georgia's room, they told her; no one had touched it since her departure.

On the wood of the door, invisible to everyone else, Naya saw a handprint made of a very soft silver light, old, almost faded, like the trace of a star that had come knocking before it left.

She did not yet know what it was.

But she knew, in that instant, that she would not be going back to Kpassè.
