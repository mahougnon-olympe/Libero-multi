# Chapter 22: The Return to Toko

On the road north, there was Toko.

Jordan had not been back in years, and Naya saw clearly, from the king's hands clenched on his reins, that this village was not a village like the others. It was in Toko, she had been told, that the cruel prince of old had begun his penance, ten years long, mending roofs and digging wells under the orders of an old chief named Dah Gbeto.

Dah Gbeto had died long ago. It was his son, Ayidji, who led the village now, a solid man with an honest gaze, who embraced Jordan the way one embraces a brother.

"King or no king," he said, "here you remain the man who redid my mother's roof. Come in, eat, and tell me what misfortune brings you, because I can see from your faces that this is no festive visit."

They ate, and they told. The green mark, Kadel, the student with the grey notebook. As Jordan spoke, Naya, seated across from Ayidji, saw the chief's face change, cloud over, then freeze entirely when Jordan spoke the name.

"Sètondji," Ayidji repeated in a strange voice. "A child of the Village of the Forgotten."

He rose slowly and walked to the window, from which one could see, at the end of the village, the great white building of the school, the very first Jordan had ever built.

"Jordan. There is something you must see."

They crossed the village to the school. In the great hall, on the far wall, the masters had painted, over the years, the roll of honour: the names of the best pupils of each generation, those Toko was proud of, those held up as examples to the little ones.

Ayidji raised his lamp. At the very top of the list, written larger than the others, a name.

Sètondji, son of the Forgotten. The most brilliant pupil this school has ever known.

"He arrived here at twelve," Ayidji recounted in a low voice. "His village school had sent him to us because he had outgrown all his masters. In two years, he outgrew ours as well. He read everything, he remembered everything. In the evenings, he stayed last, copying entire books into his grey notebooks. The masters said: this child is proof that the king's schools are saving the kingdom. And I first among them, Jordan: I said it first."

Jordan did not reply. He was looking at the name on the wall, and Naya, who saw so many invisible things, needed no magic to see this one: a man discovering that the remedy he had spent his life preparing had fed the poison.

"What I built to repair," Jordan said at last, in a voice she had never heard from him, "trained the one who destroys."

"No," Ayidji answered forcefully. "Your school taught him to read; it did not teach him to take. A thousand children have sat on these benches and become healers, teachers, mothers and fathers. One single one became this. You will not erase a thousand goods with a single evil, Jordan: that arithmetic is Kadel's."

The king stayed silent a long moment. Then he nodded slowly, though no one could tell whether he was convinced or simply tired of carrying.

Before their departure the next day, Ayidji led them into his father's house. There, in a chest Dah Gbeto had once had sealed, slept an object wrapped in white cloth. Ayidji unwrapped it with respect: a dagger with an ivory blade, engraved with ancient signs, which seemed to drink the morning light.

"After the war of the lake, Georgia entrusted it to my father. She said: keep it far from kings and sorcerers, and give it on the day someone needs it for the right reason. My father made me swear to recognise that day."

He turned, and to everyone's surprise, it was not to Jordan that he held out the dagger.

It was to Naya.

"You who see what others do not," he said, "my heart tells me you are the one who will know where to cut."

Naya took the ivory dagger. The instant her fingers touched the handle, her extra eye opened wider than it had ever opened, and she saw what no tale had ever told about this blade.

The dagger was not made to cut flesh.

It was made to cut threads.
