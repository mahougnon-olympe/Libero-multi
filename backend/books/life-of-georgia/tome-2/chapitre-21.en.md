# Chapter 21: The Villages That Fell Silent

They left the valley of the Three Villages fifteen strong: King Jordan, Naya, twelve trusted soldiers, and an old sergeant named Tovi who knew the north like his own pocket.

Jordan had wanted to raise an army. Naya had talked him out of it, and it was the first time in her life she had given advice to a king.

"Majesty, an army can be seen from far away. And the one we seek has eyes in every village: every marked person may be a sentinel. Better a small troop and my eye. I will see his spies before they see us."

Jordan had looked at her a moment, then smiled for the first time since the stone prison.

"Georgia would have said exactly the opposite. She would have charged ahead alone. Perhaps it is a good sign that you are different."

They went up toward the north following the trail of the goods. For that was the discovery of the first days: the carts. In every village they crossed, Naya spotted one or two marked inhabitants, always people of strength or trade, a blacksmith, a farmer, a carpenter, and all were doing the same thing: producing, loading, shipping northward. Grain, iron, wood, cloth. The whole kingdom, without knowing it, was feeding something.

"It is not an army he is building," Jordan understood one evening, bent over the map. "It is a capital."

But the most troubling thing, Naya discovered by drawing. Every evening, she marked on Sergeant Tovi's map the villages where she had seen the marked. On the sixth evening, she set down her charcoal and went still.

"Majesty, come and see."

The marked villages formed an arc of a circle. And extending the arc, imagining the circle complete, at its centre lay not the fortress of the north, not Black-Fish, but a point Jordan recognised before he even read the name on the map, because that point was carved into his memory forever.

"The fairy lake," he breathed. "He is encircling the lake."

No one slept much that night. And the following night, no one slept at all.

They had halted in a village called Danko, peaceful in appearance, where Naya had spotted three marked: the chief himself, his brother, and the miller. She had advised camping outside the village, under the trees, and Jordan had listened to her. That is what saved them.

In the middle of the night, Naya woke with a start, not knowing why. Then she knew: through her closed eyelids, she had seen green threads moving.

She opened her eyes. They were there, between the trees, some thirty men and women advancing on the camp in absolute silence, without a torch, without a shouted order, each tied to the sky by their quivering green thread. Villagers of Danko and elsewhere, ordinary faces with empty gazes, armed with hoes, machetes and sticks.

"Up!" Naya shouted. "Up, they are coming from the east and from the river!"

The fight that followed was the strangest Jordan's soldiers had ever fought. For the king had given a terrible order before even the first blow:

"These are innocents. Nobody kills. Nobody."

So they fought with the odds upside down, fifteen against thirty, trying to knock out without wounding opponents who, for their part, struck to kill, without a cry, without a hesitation, without ever blinking. Naya ran through the night between the soldiers, and her eye made the difference at every instant.

"Behind you, sergeant, the tall one with the hoe! That one, leave him, he is not marked, he is only fleeing! The two near the fire, careful, they are pretending to have fallen!"

By dawn, it was over. The marked were bound, unconscious or fled, and no death weighed on anyone. The soldiers looked at Naya differently now. Sergeant Tovi himself brought her the first flatcake of the morning, which, in his language, was worth a medal.

Jordan, for his part, contemplated the bound prisoners who, even tied, strained at their ropes toward the north, all in the same direction, like needles of a single compass.

"He knows we are coming," he said. "Now, he knows."
