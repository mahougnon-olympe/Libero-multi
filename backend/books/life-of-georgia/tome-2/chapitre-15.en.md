# Chapter 15: The Road and the Mark

The first days of travel taught Naya two things: freedom tastes of the wind, and fear sleeps in the same bed as freedom.

By day, she walked the western road, greeted the farmers in the fields, and ate the flatcakes her grandmother had slipped into her bag. By night, she slept in the trees, as an old hunter she met on the second day had taught her, because roads are safe for no one, and even less so for a girl alone.

She also discovered that her extra eye, on the road, became a precious friend. When a merchant offered to take her by cart to the next village, she saw the grey smoke and politely refused. When an innkeeper offered her an extra plate saying it cost nothing, she saw no smoke, and accepted with a smile. For the first time in her life, seeing lies did not cut her off from people. It protected her.

On the fifth day, she arrived in a village called Sèmè, and that is where everything changed.

Sèmè was a village like any other, with its market, its goats and its children running everywhere. But on the square, near the well, a man caught Naya's eye. He was a farmer, tall and strong, drawing water without stopping, bucket after bucket, to fill a barrel set on a cart. He did not speak. He did not wipe his brow. He looked at no one. His movements were perfect and empty, like those of a spider weaving.

And around his wrist, Naya saw a mark.

It was neither the grey smoke of lies, nor the golden threads of charms, nor the blue mist of healers. It was a ring of green light, thick, tight around the wrist like a rope, and from that ring rose a very fine green thread that climbed into the sky and vanished in the distance, to the west, like the string of an invisible kite.

"Excuse me, madam," Naya asked an orange seller, "that man at the well, is he ill?"

"Ah, poor Gbènou," the woman sighed. "Nobody knows. Two moons ago, he was the most talkative man in Sèmè. One morning he got up, and he never said another word. He works from sunrise to sunset, loads his cart, and heads west to deliver who-knows-what to who-knows-whom. His wife has consulted three healers. None of them found anything."

"And nobody saw anything... on his wrist?"

"On his wrist?" The woman gave Naya an odd look. "There is nothing on his wrist, child."

Naya thanked her, bought an orange to keep herself in countenance, and walked away, her heart pounding.

She sat in the shade of the village kapok tree, tore a piece from her wrap, and with a bit of charcoal picked up near a fire pit, she carefully drew the mark: the ring, and the small sign repeated all around it, like writing. She could not read the letters of men, but she could draw what her eyes saw, and her eyes saw better than any eyes in the kingdom.

Setting off again, she passed the cart of Gbènou the farmer leaving the village, loaded with barrels, sacks of millet and iron tools. The man held the reins, his gaze fixed straight ahead, and the green thread above his head quivered gently, stretched toward the west.

Toward the west. Like her.

Naya clenched the piece of cloth in her fist and quickened her pace. She kept telling herself it was nothing, that a healer in the big towns would know how to explain it, that it was none of her business.

But deep inside her, a small voice that sounded like her grandmother's whispered something else.

Your eye has not yet found its use, said the voice. The day it does, you will understand.

Naya walked faster.
