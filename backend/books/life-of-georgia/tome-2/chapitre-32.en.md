# Chapter 32: What We Pass On

Georgia took a whole season to heal, and during that season she did something she had never done: nothing.

She slept in her father's house in Aboula, ate her aunts' cooking, listened to the rain on the roof. In the early days, every noise outside made her leap up, ready to run and protect someone, and every time, someone in her family laid a hand on her shoulder and said gently: the world is holding, Georgia. Sit back down. It took weeks for her to believe them. A year in the cocoon had taught her what fifteen years of glory had not: what happens when you carry everything, all the time, all alone.

So, when she was herself again, she did not resume her patrols.

She had a house built, a large bright house between Aboula and the road, with a courtyard, a well and a kapok tree. And when people asked her if it was a barracks, she laughed.

"No. It is a school."

But not a school like Jordan's, which taught reading and counting. Georgia's school was for the others. For those who saw grey smoke, for those who heard their neighbours' dreams, for those who spoke to bees, for those who guessed the rain three days ahead, for all the children of the kingdom who were called cursed, witches, possessed, and who were married off by force or driven out to extinguish what they were. The rumour ran the roads faster than the carts: there is a place where having a strange gift is not a shame. They came first one by one, then by whole families.

And the very first pupil, the one who helped raise the walls and choose the mats, was naturally a seventeen-year-old girl from Kpassè, with a necklace of blue beads and an ivory dagger she kept in a chest, waiting for the day she would never need it again.

Naya wrote to her grandmother Abavo by every traveller heading east. One day, an answer arrived, dictated to the village scribe, and it held in a single sentence: I told you so: your eye has found its use; now teach them all that the rain makes things grow. Her father Codjo never wrote. Some doors take longer than others, and some never reopen, and Naya learned to live with that, because you cannot cut every thread in the world, least of all those of the heart.

As for Jordan, he kept the promise made to a wall, one night, in a ravine.

It happened on the shore of the fairy lake, where Georgia had come to thank the guardians, and where the king arrived, as if by chance, fifteen years late. The small lights danced above the silver water, round and bright as never before, and it is said they pretended not to listen, which is the greatest politeness a fairy is capable of. No one ever knew exactly what was said on that shore. It is only known that when the two silhouettes came back toward the camp, they walked side by side, unhurried, and that Georgia's old father, seeing them arrive, murmured: at last, and went back to his soup.

The years passed as good years pass, without a story.

The school of the kapok tree grew. Unheard-of things were seen there: a little boy who mended broken bones by singing, twin girls who dreamed the same dream in turns, and a schoolmistress with extraordinary eyes, whom people came to consult from the four corners of the kingdom whenever a lie, a spell or an injustice needed to be seen. Georgia grew older in the courtyard, among the children, and her hair now glowed in the dark only to make them laugh.

And then, one dry-season evening, there was a knock at the school gate.

Naya, now a grown woman, went to open it. Before her stood a little girl of about twelve, dusty, exhausted, a bundle on her shoulder, arrived alone at the end of a road one could tell had been very long. The child raised eyes full of fear and defiance, and recited in one breath the sentence she must have rehearsed the whole journey:

"I was told that here, you take in cursed girls. Back home, when I touch people, I feel their sorrows, and everyone says it is the devil, and they wanted to... I left. Is it true that here you take them in?"

Naya looked at her, and she saw, as in a mirror, a night in Kpassè, a necklace of blue beads, and an old woman saying: your eye has not yet found its use.

She stepped back from the gate and smiled.

"Come in," she said. "You have come at a good time. We were about to eat."
