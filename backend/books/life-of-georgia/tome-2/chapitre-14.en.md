# Chapter 14: The Arranged Marriage

Naya's father was named Codjo, and Codjo had not smiled like that in years.

"Rejoice, my daughter," he announced before the whole assembled family. "The village chief has asked for your hand for his son Damien. The wedding will take place at the next moon."

Naya's mother cried out with joy. The aunts applauded. The cousins immediately began talking about wraps and jewellery. In the whole courtyard there was only one person who was not rejoicing, and it was the one being married off.

Naya knew Damien. Everyone in Kpassè knew Damien, the chief's son, handsome as a feast day, polite with the elders, generous at the market. But Naya was the only one who saw the grey smoke that came out of his mouth with almost every sentence. Damien lied the way other people breathe. She had seen him swear faithfulness to three different girls in a single week, and the grey smoke had come out three times.

"Father," she said softly, "I do not want to marry Damien."

"What you want does not matter," Codjo replied without even looking at her. "It is an honour for the family. Thanks to you, perhaps people will finally stop pointing at us."

"He lies, Father. He lies all the time. I see it."

"Enough!"

The word cracked like a dry branch. Codjo had risen to his feet, and the whole courtyard had gone silent.

"That is exactly what I am talking about," he said in a low, hard voice. "Your visions. Your smoke. Your stories. All my life I have carried the shame of having a daughter the village takes for a witch. The chief is the only man in Kpassè willing to overlook it. So you will marry Damien, you will close that extra eye of yours, and you will finally become a normal woman."

Naya looked at her father, and for the first time in her life, she would have preferred to see the grey smoke. But there was none. Codjo was not lying. He meant every word.

That was what hurt her most.

She did not cry in front of them. She waited for night, and when everyone was asleep, she walked to the last hut of the village, near the great kapok tree. Her grandmother's lamp was still burning, as if the old woman were expecting her.

"I know," said Abavo before Naya even opened her mouth. "The whole village knows. Sit down."

Naya sat, and this time she wept. Her grandmother let her, because there are waters you must let flow before you can cross them. Then, when the storm had passed, the old woman rose, rummaged in a calabash on the highest shelf, and drew out a necklace of blue beads, worn, ancient, which seemed to have travelled more than all the inhabitants of Kpassè put together.

"Listen to me well, little one. Far away from here, to the west, there is a village called Aboula. And in Aboula lives a woman whose story you know, for it was told to you at bedtime."

"Georgia?" Naya breathed. "The girl who breathes fire? Grandmother, that is a fairy tale."

"I too am a fairy tale to those who have never met me," Abavo replied with a smile. "Georgia exists. She too, at your age, fled a marriage. She too had a gift her village took for a curse. If anyone in this world can understand your eye, it is her."

She placed the necklace around Naya's neck.

"It belonged to my own grandmother, who also saw. Leave tonight. Follow the western road, and do not look back."

Naya hugged the old woman so tightly she could feel her tired heart beating.

"And you, Grandmother?"

"Me, I am too old for the roads, but old enough to stand up to your father. Go."

That night, like another young girl fifteen years before her, Naya left her village with a small bag, a necklace of blue beads, and a great deal of courage.

She did not know what awaited her on the western road.

She did not yet know that the road was waiting for her.
