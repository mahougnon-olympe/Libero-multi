# Chapter 30: The Girl Without Magic

At dawn on the third day, the north caught fire.

The supers of Aboula struck the main gate of the fortress like a storm. The sister with hands of steel tore out the portcullises, the brother swift as the wind whirled along the ramparts disarming the sentinels, Jordan's soldiers sounded all their trumpets, and the king himself, in armour, led the assault, shouting louder than his weariness. It was a magnificent din, a calculated chaos, and it was working: to the eye of Naya, hidden in the eastern ravine, all the threads of the fortress had turned toward the battle like ears of grain toward the sun.

She slipped along the ravine, crossed the breach in the old wall, and arrived before the first veil.

Seen up close, it was a wall of green mist, immense, rising to the sky. Naya took a deep breath, thought of her grandmother Abavo, and stepped into it.

The veil passed over her like cool water. Nothing. Not an alarm, not a shiver of the weave. She was so empty of magic that magic could not see her. All her life she had been told she had nothing, and that nothing made her invisible to the eyes of the god of this place. She almost laughed, and she almost cried, and she did both later, but not now.

Second veil. Third. Empty corridors, the sentinels gone to the din. And at last, the great doors, and the hall of the cocoon.

It turned slowly, enormous, purring, drinking in the light of the world. Naya came close enough to touch it. Through the thousands of threads, she saw the sleeping woman, her floating hair, her peaceful, false face.

"Georgia," she called. "Georgia, listen to me."

Nothing. The dream was deep, designed by a methodical man. Then Naya remembered the vial, the last water of the fairy lake. If one day you doubt what you see, the witch had said. She poured one drop on each of her eyelids.

The world exploded with clarity. The entire weave appeared to her, every thread, every knot, and at the heart of the cocoon she finally saw the truth: a tiny thread, silver, almost extinguished, linking Georgia's heart to the surface. The channel of the dream. The door of voices. Naya pressed her hands against it and spoke into it the way one speaks through a door.

"Georgia! My name is Naya, you do not know me. I am sixteen, I come from Kpassè, in the east, and I fled a marriage like you!"

Deep in the light, the sleeping face stirred.

"Listen to me! Everything you see around you in your dream, the people you protect, the people who need you, it is false! He built that dream out of your finest quality! He makes you burn for shadows, and every flame feeds his walls!"

The sleeper's eyelids trembled. The cocoon, all around, began to spin faster, uneasy.

"Georgia, you must do one thing, and it is the hardest anyone has ever asked of you. You must let go. Put out your fire. All your fire. Stop fighting, stop protecting, stop carrying. Make yourself useless! The cocoon drinks your strength: if you are nothing but Georgia with nothing, it will starve!"

And from the depths of the dream, for the first time, a voice answered, distant, exhausted, stubborn:

"If I let go... who will hold?"

Naya smiled through her tears, because Jordan was right, and because she knew the answer by heart; she had learned it on every road since Kpassè.

"We will! Your father is breaking down the great gate! Your sister is tearing out the walls! Jordan has been screaming your name under the ramparts since morning! The fairies are holding, Toko is holding, Aboula is holding, and I am here, at the foot of your cocoon, with the ivory dagger and one eye too many! You carried us for fifteen years, Georgia! Once, just once, let yourself be carried!"

A long silence. The cocoon turned and turned.

Then, at the heart of the light, the fire went out.

It was not an explosion. It was the opposite: an immense exhalation, as if the whole world were letting its shoulders drop. The thousands of threads, cut off from their source, paled, trembled, and the cocoon collapsed in on itself in a rain of dying sparks. Naya leapt forward and caught in her arms a woman with long hair, thin, burning with fever, alive.

Georgia opened her eyes, and her first gaze as a free woman fell on an unknown girl of sixteen.

"Was that you, the voice?" she murmured.

"It was me."

"You have one eye too many," Georgia said in a breath that resembled a smile. "My favourite of all curses."

At the same instant, for leagues around, every green thread in the kingdom snapped at once. On the ramparts, the army of the marked stopped dead and woke up weeping. Above the distant lake, hundreds of small lights, freed from the net, burst from the silver water like a flight of furious stars and streaked north.

And in his tower, bent over his grey notebooks, Sètondji watched his whole work go out line after line, and he understood, with his perfect lucidity, exactly what was coming.

The fairies were coming to take back what belonged to them.
