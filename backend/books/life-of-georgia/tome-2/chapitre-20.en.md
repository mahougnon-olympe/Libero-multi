# Chapter 20: Nobody's Student

Far to the north, in a fortress rebuilt on ruins, a young man was writing by the light of a lamp that burned no oil.

His name was Sètondji, and you had to go back a long way to understand how anyone arrives there.

Sètondji was born in the Village of the Forgotten, that village nobody wanted, peopled by families driven out from elsewhere. He was born on the night of the great battle of the lake, his mother said, the night the western sky turned red and gold. He grew up without a father, in mud and hunger, and he grew up above all on stories. In the evenings, around the fire, the elders of the Forgotten told the war of the lake better than anyone, because they had seen everything pass along their road: the refugees, the soldiers, the monsters, and at the end, the victory of others.

For that was what little Sètondji had kept, sitting in the shadows, knees hugged to his chest: the victory of others.

When King Jordan had schools built across the kingdom to redeem his faults, a school was born near the Village of the Forgotten, and Sètondji entered it at eight years old. There he discovered two things that decided his entire life. The first: he was more intelligent than everyone, masters included. The second: intelligence without power serves only to see more clearly what the world is made of.

And the world, as Sètondji saw it, was made of negligence.

He had redone the accounts, line by line, in his grey notebooks. Kadel had stolen powers for years before anyone seriously cared. How many villages burned in that time? The fairies of the lake, keepers of all the magic in the world, knew. They had waited to be sought out, seated on their silver water, while children slept in the mud. Georgia had won, yes; then she had gone home to protect one village, a single one, her own. Jordan had built schools, that was good, and the schools produced scribes and dreamers while magic, the true strength of the world, remained scattered by the accident of birth, given to farmers who did nothing with it and denied to those who would have known what to do.

Good existed; Sètondji did not deny it. But good was amateur. Good slept, aged, went home. Evil had lost only because it was as badly organised as good.

So Sètondji had devised the solution, and he had written it at the head of his first notebook, at fourteen, in careful handwriting: magic must no longer belong to anyone, so that it may belong to everyone. It must be gathered in a single point, kept by a single will that never sleeps, never ages, never goes home.

A single will. His own. Not out of pride, he told himself, but because he had searched honestly for someone more capable, and had found no one.

The rest had been mere work. Finding old Kadel and drinking in his science while playing the student. Correcting the master's errors, one by one. Inverting the great spell, for Sètondji's genius came down to one inversion: Kadel gave his mark in order to control, which exhausted him. Sètondji had learned to drain, to draw magic out of the beings who had it, to store it, concentrate it, hold it.

That evening, in the rebuilt fortress, he closed his notebook and went down to the great hall. At its centre, a cocoon of light turned slowly on itself, tall as three men, woven of thousands of green and gold threads. He laid his hand on it, and the cocoon purred like a well-fed beast.

"Soon," he said gently. "Soon no one will sleep in the mud anymore."

Inside the cocoon, a silhouette floated, asleep, her long hair rippling in the light like waterweed in a current.

Sètondji looked at her with something that sincerely resembled gratitude.

"Thank you for all you are giving, protector. You see: in the end, you are saving the world after all."
