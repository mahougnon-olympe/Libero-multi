# Chapter 17: What the Old Witch Knew

The next morning, Georgia's father woke Naya before the sun.

"There is someone you must see," he said. "If anyone can understand your eye and this mark, it is her. She is the woman who set my daughter on her path, fifteen years ago."

He gave her a guide, a donkey, and three days' provisions. The old witch no longer lived in her old roadside hut: grown too old, she had settled in a cool cave at the foot of the hills, where grateful villagers brought her food.

When Naya entered the cave, she thought at first there was no one there. Then a voice came out of the shadows, dry and amused like old wood creaking.

"Fourteen days' walk from the east, a necklace of blue beads, and one eye too many. Come closer, little one. I have waited sixty years to meet someone like you."

The witch was tiny, bent double with age, but her eyes glowed like two embers no rain had ever managed to put out. She sat Naya down, took her face between her cold hands, and looked at her for a long, long time.

"So it is true," she murmured at last. "True sight. I thought that gift extinct since my own grandmother. Little one, do you know what you are?"

"A girl everyone runs from," Naya answered.

"A weave-seer," the witch corrected. "Listen to me well. The world is woven of threads of magic, like a cloth. Sorcerers like me know how to pull on a few threads, but we work blind, by touch, by memory. You, you see the whole fabric. Lies, which tear the weave. Spells, which knot it. Curses, which pull it tight. One weave-seer is born in a century, when the century is lucky. And none is ever born for nothing."

Naya felt a shiver run down her back. She took out the piece of wrap and unfolded it before the old woman.

"Then tell me what this is."

The witch looked at the drawing, and her face closed all at once, as the cave itself seemed to darken.

"Where?"

"A farmer, in Sèmè. The mark was on his wrist, and a green thread ran off toward the west. No one else could see it."

"Of course no one could see it," the witch said in a hollow voice. "Kadel branded his victims with the iron of his magic, and his magic was taken from him by the fairies down to the last drop. I verified it myself. What you saw is not Kadel's work."

"Then whose?"

"Someone who learned from him. And that is far worse."

The old woman rose painfully and walked to the back of the cave, where she kept a chest of black wood.

"A year ago, a woman came and sat exactly where you are sitting. Long hair, a gaze that burned, and the same drawing as yours, traced on a leaf. She had seen it on the wrist of a woodcutter, near the Wassa forest. She could not understand either how the mark could exist."

"Georgia," Naya breathed.

"Georgia. I told her what I am telling you: what is returning is not Kadel. Kadel was a man broken by birth, driven by hatred, and hatred always ends up making mistakes. What is returning is young, patient and methodical. It does not have the old sorcerer's rage. It has taken notes. I told her not to go alone."

The witch gave a sad laugh.

"You might as well tell the rain to fall upward. She left the next day for the north, alone, as always. I never saw her again."

She opened the black chest, took out a small glass vial filled with faintly glowing water, and placed it in Naya's hands.

"Water from the fairy lake, the last I possess. It will not give you power; your power is enough. But if one day you doubt what you see, pour one drop on your eyelids, and the weave will appear to you clearer than daylight. Now hear my advice and my warning."

"I am listening."

"The advice: go to Black-Fish, find King Jordan, show him your mark. He alone has carried that sign in his flesh; he alone will know to believe you. The warning: the one you are going to seek may already see that you see him. A weave-seer is the only true danger to a weave-weaver. If he learns that you exist, little one, he will not mark you. He will erase you."

Naya pressed the vial to her heart, bowed, and stepped out of the cave into the morning light.

The road to Black-Fish led north.

She took it.
