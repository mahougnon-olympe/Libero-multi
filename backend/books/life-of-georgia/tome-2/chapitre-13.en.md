# Chapter 13: The Girl Who Saw Too Much

Once upon a time, in a village called Kpassè, there lived a sixteen-year-old girl named Naya, whom everyone avoided.

It was not because she was unkind. Naya was gentle, hard-working, and she had a laugh that made you want to laugh along with her. No, if the people of Kpassè lowered their eyes when she passed, it was for another reason: Naya saw too much.

When the market woman swore her tomatoes had been picked that morning, Naya saw a little grey smoke drift out of her mouth, and she knew the tomatoes were three days old. When the neighbour promised to repay his debt before the new moon, the grey smoke drifted out again, and Naya knew he would never repay it. No one else saw that smoke. No one believed her, but everyone had noticed that little Naya guessed lies the way one guesses rain, and that was enough to frighten them.

"That child has one eye too many," the old women of the village would say.

Her mother had long since stopped defending her. Her father was ashamed. Only her grandmother Abavo, who lived in the last hut of the village, near the great kapok tree, welcomed her every evening without ever lowering her eyes.

"Grandmother," Naya asked one evening, "why do I see what others cannot?"

"Because the world gave you a gift," the old woman answered, pounding her leaves.

"A gift? People love gifts. Me, they run from."

"People run from the rain too," said Abavo. "And yet without it, nothing grows. Your eye has not yet found its use, that is all. The day it does, you will understand."

Naya sighed. Her grandmother always spoke in images, and images are no comfort to a sixteen-year-old girl who eats alone at the edge of the field.

But what Naya told no one, not even Abavo, was that for some weeks now she had been seeing something other than the grey smoke of lies. Sometimes, on certain travellers passing through Kpassè, she glimpsed very fine threads of light, like coloured spiderwebs, clinging to their clothes or their skin. A peddler wore a golden thread around his neck, at the exact spot where his charm hung. A woman coming back from the healer's had her hands wrapped in a very pale blue mist, which faded in three days, along with her illness.

Naya had come to understand, without daring to say it aloud: she did not only see lies. She saw magic.

Now magic, in Kpassè, was spoken of only in whispers. The elders told how, fifteen years ago, far away to the west, a terrible sorcerer had been defeated on the shore of a silver lake by a village girl who breathed fire. It was said that this girl still lived, that her name was Georgia, and that her hair glowed in the dark when she was angry. The mothers of Kpassè told this story to their children at bedtime, and the children fell asleep dreaming that one day they too would be strong enough that no one could ever hurt them again.

Naya did not dream of breathing fire.

She dreamed only of a place where having one eye too many would not be a curse.

She did not yet know that no such place existed, and that she would have to build it herself. But before that, a door had to close so that a road could open.

And the door closed the very next day, at sunset, when her father came home from the village council with great news and a great smile.
