# Chapter 31: The Trial of the Builder

The fairies made no speeches.

They swept through the fortress like a storm of light, ignoring the soldiers, ignoring the weeping marked, and they found Sètondji in his tower, standing amid his notebooks, and he did not flee. There was a white flash seen from the hills, and when Jordan and the supers reached the tower, they found a young man kneeling, intact, eyes open, and as empty of magic as an upturned calabash. The guardians of the lake had taken back every last stolen drop, and with it all the power of the man who had dared to net them.

The trial took place a month later, in Black-Fish, and the whole kingdom held its breath, for everyone remembered the first trial, Kadel's, and everyone expected the same course: the crimes, the sentence, the penance.

But Jordan had spent fifteen years thinking about the first trial, and he imposed on the tribunal something no one had foreseen.

"We will judge his acts," he said at the opening, "and they will be punished. But this time, we will not stop there. Fifteen years ago, we sentenced Kadel and we went home, relieved, without ever asking ourselves how a child born without power had been able to become Kadel. The result: we saw nothing coming when it began again. So this time, before closing this case, this tribunal will answer one more question. Not only: what did he do? But: how was this possible?"

And the trial became something other than a trial.

They heard the elders of the Village of the Forgotten tell of the mud, the hunger, and the stares of the other villages. They heard the masters of Toko tell of the brilliant pupil everyone admired and no one had truly known, the child first everywhere and invited nowhere. They heard Naya tell of the offer in the fortress, and her question that had gone unanswered: in your perfect world, who will have the right to tell you no?

And then, on the fifth day, came the testimony no one would forget.

Kadel entered the hall, under escort, stooped, his mason's hands crossed before him, and he came to stand facing his student. The two fallen sorcerers looked at each other a long time, the old one who had destroyed out of hatred and the young one who had destroyed out of love of the good, and the whole hall understood that it was witnessing something greater than a trial.

"You lied to me for seven years," Kadel said at last.

"I never lied to you," Sètondji replied with his perfect sincerity. "I truly wanted to understand your errors. I understood them. I corrected them."

"No," said the old man. "You corrected my methods. My errors: you repeated them all, one by one. I believed the world owed me reparation; you believed the world owed you obedience. It is the same error dressed differently: believing that a single pain, or a single intelligence, weighs more than the freedom of everyone else. Look at me well, boy. I had your certainties. Here is where they lead: forty years of stones to carry, and faces that do not fade at night."

Sètondji held his gaze, and for the first time since his arrest, something wavered in his eyes. Not remorse, not yet. A doubt. The very first.

The sentence fell on the seventh day: the same as his master's, for Jordan insisted on that justice. Sètondji would rebuild with his own hands, without magic, village after village, everything his marked had been forced to build and everything his war had broken, beginning with the Village of the Forgotten, where decent houses would at last replace the mud.

But the tribunal also handed down a second verdict, with no guilty party and without precedent, which the scribes wrote into the law of the kingdom: never again would a child of the kingdom grow up in a village nobody wanted. The Forgotten would receive land, wells and a school. And in all the king's schools, they would no longer teach only reading and counting, but also the thing the kingdom's most brilliant pupil had never learned: that an intelligence that listens to no one always ends up believing itself alone in the world, and it always ends up being right, alone, amid the ruins.

Leaving the tribunal, Naya walked for a while at the side of Georgia, still thin and wrapped in a shawl, who had watched everything from the back of the hall.

"A trial that ends with a question," said Naya. "It is strange."

"No," Georgia answered, looking at the sky. "It is the first time we have truly won."
