# Chapter 11: The Fairies' Water

The bolt never touched Jordan.

The instant it struck, a silver light burst from the lake and wrapped the two young people like a dome. Kadel's green bolt crashed against it and went out, absorbed without a sound.

The fairies rose above the water, in their hundreds, and their light grew as they climbed. A voice then rang out, seeming to come from everywhere at once, from the lake, from the sky, from the flowers:

"For a thousand years, no impure heart has been able to approach these waters. And for a thousand years, never have we seen this: a bewitched heart that chooses to die to save another. The spell that gnaws at you has no more hold on you, child. You defeated it yourself, the instant you turned around."

The dome of light dissolved gently. Jordan, incredulous, looked at his hands: Kadel's green mark, which he had worn on his wrist for a month, was fading like ink under the rain.

"NO!" screamed Kadel. "That's impossible! This is MY work!"

The sorcerer threw himself forward, gathering his last strength for one final spell. But the fairies were faster. Their lights swooped down on him like a swarm and wrapped him in a silver cocoon. Kadel screamed, struggled, then his voice weakened, weakened... and fell silent.

When the lights scattered, all that remained of the terrible sorcerer was an old man on his knees, stripped of all magic, staring at his empty hands without understanding.

"We do not kill," said the voice of the fairies. "But we take back what has been corrupted. Your magic returns to the earth, Kadel. You will live out the rest of your days with the only power you have left: that of remembering what you have done."

At that very moment, all across the land, the shadow creatures froze, shuddered, and came apart into smoke, freeing those they had once been. In the village of Toko, a woman with a round face saw a young man stagger out of the mist, and she recognized her son.

But at the edge of the lake, Jordan had eyes only for Georgia. She was barely breathing. He lifted her and walked into the water up to his waist.

"Please," he begged. "The lake was supposed to heal me. I don't need it anymore. Heal her instead. Take my healing, take whatever you want, but save her."

"Your healing is no longer ours to give, it is already within you," answered the voice. "But lean down, child. The water itself decides whom it saves."

Jordan let a little water flow between Georgia's lips.

One second. Two. Three.

Then the ash-gray hair stirred. One lock turned brown again, then another, then all of them, silkier and more radiant than ever. Color returned to her cheeks. And Georgia opened her eyes.

The first thing she saw was Jordan's face, streaming with tears, framed by a sky where hundreds of little lights danced.

"I'm still dreaming," she murmured.

"No," said Jordan, laughing and crying at once. "It's over. We did it. You did it."

Georgia looked around: the silver lake, the fairies, Kadel kneeling on the shore under the guard of a sphere of light. Then she lowered her eyes to Jordan's wrist, where the green mark had vanished.

"Your heart?" she asked.

"Mine. Mine alone. For better and for worse."

She nodded slowly. Then, with a small tired smile:

"Then get me out of this water, Prince. It's freezing, and I have a witch to thank, a family to find again, and a trial to prepare."
