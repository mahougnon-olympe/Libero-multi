# Chapter 8: The Village of the Forgotten

Georgia slept a whole day and a whole night. When she opened her eyes again, she was no longer in the valley: she was lying on a mat, in a thatched-roof hut, and the smell of hot soup drifted in the air.

"Ah, Sleeping Beauty awakes!" called out a cheerful voice.

A woman of a certain age, with a round face and laughing eyes, handed her a steaming bowl. Jordan was sitting in a corner, his features drawn with fatigue.

"You carried me all the way here?" Georgia asked, astonished.

"For six hours," the woman clarified before Jordan could answer. "He refused to stop until he'd found help. Stubborn as a mule, this one."

The village was called Toko, but its inhabitants called it the Village of the Forgotten. For everyone who lived there had one thing in common: they had all, one day, crossed paths with Kadel.

"There's Sena, whom Kadel made mute because she sang too close to his tower. There's old Codjo, whose fields he burned. And me," said the woman, lowering her eyes, "he took my son. Kadel turned him into one of his shadow creatures. My boy is perhaps one of those you fought in the valley."

A heavy silence fell over the hut. Jordan clenched his fists.

"Why has no one ever stopped him?" he asked.

"Because those who could have, didn't," answered a voice from the doorway.

An old man stood in the entrance, leaning on a carved staff. He was the village chief, Dah Gbeto.

"I was there, twenty years ago, when the king banished Kadel," he continued. "What the sorcerer doesn't tell is what his failed experiment was: he was trying to create a potion of immortality for the royal family, and he tested it on villagers. Seven people died. Your father banished him for that, Prince Jordan. He should have had him locked away, but Kadel was his friend, so he took pity. And his pity condemned us all."

Jordan stayed silent for a long time. Then he stood and faced the villagers who had gathered.

"I cannot undo what my father failed to do. But I can make you a promise. We are going to the fairy lake to break the spell within me. And when it is done, I will come back. Not as a prince paying a visit. As a man come to pay his family's debts. Kadel will be arrested, tried, and your names will never be forgotten again."

Dah Gbeto studied him at length with eyes clouded by age.

"I have heard many a prince's promise," he said at last. "But this one has something different about it. It doesn't come from the mouth. It comes from a deeper place."

The chief struck the ground with his staff.

"You will leave tomorrow at dawn. Sena will prepare provisions for you. And take this."

He handed Jordan an old dagger with an ivory handle.

"It belonged to the first chief of Toko. They say its blade is the only metal Kadel's shadows cannot pass through. It won't kill the sorcerer, but it can protect you, you who have no powers."

That night, lying near the fire, Georgia watched Jordan sharpening the dagger by the light of the flames. She thought back to the arrogant boy who had trapped her in his castle, then to the one who had carried her for six hours without stopping.

The witch was right, she thought. The darkest hearts sometimes ask only for a little light.

But she also knew the hardest part was yet to come. For in the morning, only one day's walk would remain.

One day's walk to the lake. And to Kadel.
