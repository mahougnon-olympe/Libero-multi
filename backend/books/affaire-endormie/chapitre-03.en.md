# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 3: Widow No. 3

Boulder City is the only town in Nevada where casinos are banned, which makes it, depending on your point of view, the most virtuous or the most boring in the state. Quiet streets, tidy lawns, and at 4210 Ridgecrest Lane, a cream-colored house with a porch where Ellen Teller waits for me, her hands around a cup she won't drink from.

I called last night, from my living room, under the gaze of the ten pinned sheets. Technically, I'm not allowed to be here. Technically, I'm digitizing boxes twenty-five miles from here. I've decided to consider my presence in Boulder City a matter of field digitization, a concept I reserve the right to officially invent later.

"You're younger than the ones before," she says by way of hello.

She must be nearing sixty, elegant in the way of women who decided one day to stand upright and stuck to it. She sits me on the porch, facing the garden. On the windowsill, a frame: Marcus Teller, tanned, laughing, a fish held out at arm's length.

"Thank you for seeing me, Mrs. Teller. I know my approach is unusual. The case is officially closed."

"The case, maybe." She looks at the garden. "Ask your questions, Agent Cole. I've had twelve years to practice answering them."

So I begin, gently, with what's known. Marcus, insurance broker, well liked, with no declared enemies, found one morning in April 2008. She answers with the slightly worn precision of someone who has already told it all three times to three different investigators. I let her run on, and while she speaks, I do my unofficial job: I listen to her think. There are the learned answers, smooth, and then there are the places where her voice stumbles. It stumbles twice. The first when she mentions the weeks before the death. The second when she says the word "mail."

I wait for the end of a sentence and set down my cup.

"Tell me about the letter."

She really looks at me for the first time.

"Someone passed that on to you? The others didn't put much stock in it."

"I believe in things that disappear, ma'am. This letter disappeared twice: from the evidence, and from the reports. That interests me at least twice over."

She sets down her own cup in turn, stands, and goes to fetch something from the house. I hear a drawer, a rustle. She comes back with an old diary, tan leather, a tired elastic band.

"It was three weeks before. March 20, I noted it there, later, when the police started asking for dates. A white envelope, no return address, mailed from Las Vegas. Inside, not a word. Just this."

She opens the diary to a page marked with a paper clip and hands me a rectangle of photographic paper. My pulse does something unusual.

It's not the seal 31 evidence, of course. It's a color photocopy, folded in four, that Marcus Teller made before handing the original to the investigators. Ellen kept it twelve years in a diary, the way you keep a splinter.

The fragment shows a corner of a photograph cut out with scissors, cleanly, following a straight line. In it you can see a piece of chain-link fence, a bit of pale asphalt, and the corner of a painted wooden sign of which only three letters remain: "...OOL." In the lower corner, a date printed in small orange figures, the kind the photo labs of old used to produce: 06 87.

June 1987.

"How did Marcus react?"

"That's the question no one asked me back then." She takes back the photocopy, smooths it with the flat of her hand. "They wanted to know where the envelope came from, who might have had it in for him, whether he had debts. But his reaction... Marcus was a cheerful man, Agent Cole. The kind to sing off-key on purpose in the car. When he opened that envelope, he went gray. Not worried. Gray. He stood ten minutes in the kitchen staring at that scrap of paper, and when I asked what it was, he said: 'Nothing. An old bit of nonsense.'"

"An old bit of nonsense. Those were his words?"

"His exact words. And that night, I heard him get up. I found him in his study at two in the morning, going through boxes of old things, his mother's belongings that we'd brought back after her death. I asked him what he was looking for. He told me he wasn't looking for anything, that he was tidying up. Marcus never tidied a thing in his life."

I take notes. Not in the official notebook I'm not allowed to have, in the other one.

"Mrs. Teller, where did your husband grow up?"

She makes a small gesture toward the north, beyond the garden.

"A place no one's ever heard of. Carver Falls. His family left when he was twelve or thirteen, when the mines closed. He practically never spoke of it." She pauses. "Why? The other investigators never asked me about his childhood."

Because the other investigators were looking for an adult motive, ma'am. Debts, affairs, contracts. No one looks for the motive of a murder in a schoolyard.

I don't say it. I say I'm casting a wide net, which has the merit of being true and the charm of being vague. But in my head, the pinned map lights up: Teller, born in Carver Falls. Plus the three others from my notebook, that makes four out of ten. And a photo fragment dated June 1987, the year Marcus Teller was eleven.

"The police kept the original, you say. Did they notice the cut?"

"They said it was probably to hide the place or the people." She pours me more tea I also don't drink. "What always struck me was the care. Look at the edge. It's not torn, it's not cut just anyhow. It's cut with a ruler. Someone took their time. Someone did this cleanly, like a job."

I look at the edge. She's right. A patient, careful cut, almost schoolroom-neat.

"May I photograph this copy?"

"You can take it." She closes the diary over the empty paper clip. "Twelve years I've kept it, telling myself that one day someone would come back for it. Here you are."

On the doorstep, as I thank her, she holds the screen door a second.

"Agent Cole. The man they convicted. Do you think it's him?"

There are trick questions, and there are questions that are doors. I choose to tell the truth, for once without wrapping.

"I think the person who sent that photo knew exactly what they were doing, ma'am. And I'm not certain that knowledge was ever presented to the jury."

She nods slowly, as if I'd just confirmed twelve years of sleepless nights, and closes the door without a sound.

On the road back, night falls over the desert and I drive with the photocopied fragment on the passenger seat, strapped under the belt like a key witness, which is ridiculous and entirely owned.

Three letters on a sign. "...OOL."

School.

I know it with a certainty that has nothing professional about it and everything to do with the tilting painting. A chain-link fence, pale asphalt, an old-style painted sign. A yard. June 1987, the end-of-year photo, the one taken in the sun before the holidays, everyone in a row, the little ones in front.

Someone cut that photo with a ruler, piece by piece, and mailed a fragment to each of them. Not a written threat, not a claim of responsibility. Just: look. Remember. You know who I am.

And Marcus Teller knew. He went gray, he searched his mother's boxes in the dead of night, and he lied to his wife for the first time in his life.

An old bit of nonsense.

I press the accelerator, not much, what the law tolerates, and I talk to myself in the car, a habit I deny in public.

"What did you do to him, Marcus? What did the ten of you do to him?"

The desert doesn't answer. It's good at that game.

Tomorrow, I'll have to find a way into a state prison without Brennan finding out. I have a convict to listen to. Twelve years Douglas Herrera has been repeating that he did nothing, and for the first time since the trial, someone is going to sit across from him considering that it might be true.
