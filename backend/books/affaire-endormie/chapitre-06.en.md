# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 6: False Lead

The weekend, I spend on Rourke. Two whole days, shutters drawn, reconstructing a man's career from what lingers in the public record: press clippings, annual reports, official commendations. Thomas Rourke, joined the office in 1996. Co-lead of the NV-0834 investigation from March 2008 to the conviction. His partner of the time retired in 2011 and died two years later, which leaves Rourke the sole custodian of this case. Promoted after the trial. Cited twice. A month from retirement, a memo about the send-off party planned for August informs me.

And I, before my wall, do what convinced men do: I file the facts in the direction of my conviction. The case got him promoted, so he protects it. He came down to the archives Friday, so he's the one who emptied the folders. He has the seniority, the access, the contacts to find Ellen Teller's number. Each piece slots in with a small satisfying click, and Monday morning, I go up to the ninth with a file under my arm and the gait of a man who believes he's got something.

I'll grant myself this: I do have something. Only, it's not what I think. It's the handle.

Brennan hears me out for eight minutes this time. The missing pages, photos to back them up. The anonymous call to the widow, prepaid number. And the register line: Consultation of 2008-2009 files, Agent T. Rourke, Friday, 4:40 p.m. I conclude by requesting the opening of an internal review. I ask calmly, without a joke, with the gravity of a man who rehearsed in his car.

Brennan looks at the copy of the register for a long time. Then he picks up his phone.

"Rourke? Come up."

What follows unfolds partly in the office, partly in front of it, door open, which guarantees the whole floor a quality broadcast. Rourke arrives, greets, listens to Brennan summarize. I watch his face, I wait for the crack, the shifting gaze, anything resembling what I built all weekend.

He frowns. He looks sincerely, deeply baffled.

"Friday 4:40 p.m.?" He turns to me, not yet angry, almost worried for me. "Son, Friday at four, I was at Simmons's medal ceremony, at the downtown auditorium. On the platform. I handed over the second decoration myself. Half the office is in the photos and there's a twenty-minute speech everyone remembers, alas for Simmons."

The silence that follows has a particular texture. Brennan taps at his keyboard, opens the internal portal, and the ceremony photo appears on his screen: Friday, time-stamped 4:47 p.m., Rourke on the platform, a medal in hand, sixty witnesses seated before him.

"Then the register is lying," I say, and I hear my own voice decide on its own to keep going. "Someone wrote your name to muddy the trail, which proves precisely that the documents were..."

"Or else," Brennan cuts in without raising his voice, which is worse, "someone who spends his days alone in that cellar wrote the name himself."

The office floor performs a slight pitching motion. I take a second too long to understand, and that second shows.

"Let's go over it," says Brennan, counting on his fingers. "An agent assigned to digitization has for two weeks been running an investigation no one authorized. He travels to a victim's widow presenting himself under his federal title. He obtains a visiting room at Ely by means of a fanciful pretext, the facility's management forwarded me the form this morning, I was waiting to understand before acting. And this same agent brings me today a falsified register accusing the most decorated veteran on this floor, who has an alibi of sixty people." He lays his hands flat. "Tell me, Cole, what I'm supposed to conclude."

I could say: that someone is framing me, and that the falsification of the register proves it. It's the truth, and it's exactly what a guilty man would say. That's the genius of the thing, I contemplate it from the inside: every proof of my good faith has become a proof against me. My evidence wasn't stolen from me. It was turned against me, one piece at a time, with the patient cut of a job done with a ruler.

"I did not write that line," I say, and I hear myself, and I hear what it's worth.

"The register is kept in a cellar where you're alone eight hours a day." Brennan stands. "Agent Cole, you are suspended from your duties as a precautionary measure, pending a review by professional responsibility. Hand over your credentials. You'll be notified."

There's a protocol for these moments, and the protocol has this mercy about it, that it keeps the hands busy. I put down the badge. I put down the card. In the hallway, no one laughs. It's the first time in eight years that I cross this floor without triggering a single smile, and I discover that the colleagues' silence weighs heavier than all their jokes combined. Delgado, by the coffee machine, opens his mouth, closes it, and looks at his cup.

It's Rourke who catches me at the elevators. He walks alongside me without a word to the doors, and there, in a low voice, for me alone:

"Twenty years ago, a young agent publicly accused his supervisor on a hunch. He'd got the wrong man. Not the wrong crime." The doors open. He doesn't get in with me. "You got the wrong door, son. That doesn't always mean you got the wrong house."

The doors close on his face, which I can no longer read, and the elevator takes me all the way down, lower than the cellar, to the floor that doesn't exist and where finished careers are stored.

The parking lot is an oven. I stay sitting in my car without turning the key, hands on the wheel, and I take stock. Suspended. Discredited. Cut off from the archives, cut off from the files, cut off from everything. Herrera will stay at Ely, the widow will stop answering, and the case will go back to sleep for another twelve years, tucked in by my own hands. I wanted to prove I could finish an investigation. I managed to finish mine.

And yet.

Sitting in that overheated car, at the bottom of the pit, something refuses to sink with the rest. One fact, a single one, bare, incontestable: someone wrote Rourke's name in that register. It wasn't me. It wasn't Rourke, sixty witnesses attest. So there is a third hand. A hand that knew I would read that register, that knew I would charge in, that measured my gullibility to the millimeter and used me like a projectile to destroy me against the first available wall.

I wasn't just framed. I was read. Someone studied my way of thinking closely enough to predict my every move, and there's only one place in the world where I could have been studied like that: two feet away from me, day after day, while I thought aloud.

I don't see the face yet. I swear I don't see it yet, at that moment, in that car. But for the first time, I feel the gaze. Attentive. Patient. That of the kid at the anthill, who has just pulled out his stick and is counting the ants in rout.

I start the engine. Suspended means without a badge, without access, without authority.

It also means: without a leash. No more Brennan, no more register, no more forms rising who knows where into hands I can't see.

I have left a notebook, a photocopy folded in four, a pinned road map, and time. The man who destroyed me thinks he stopped me.

He has only just set me free.
