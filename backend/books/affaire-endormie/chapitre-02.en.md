# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 2: Ten Names on a Wall

My apartment in Summerlin has two virtues: it's clean, and no one ever comes here. The second explains the first.

It's past ten at night and the living room wall has stopped being a wall. Ten sheets are pinned to it in two rows, one per victim, with the name, age, city, and date in black marker. I dug a Nevada road map out of the glove box and nailed it to the center, riddled with red pins. In the TV shows, the corkboard killer is the moment when his wife starts to worry. The advantage of being single: here, only the air conditioner judges me.

I step back three paces and look.

Samuel Ortiz, 37, Henderson. Dana Whitfield, 36, Reno. Marcus Teller, 38, Boulder City. Aaron Boone, 37, pastor in Pahrump. Lucille Grady, 38, nurse in Sparks. Raymond Kessler, 37, pool salesman in Mesquite. Then Novak, Aldana, Pruitt, Marsh. Ten red pins scattered over four hundred miles, from Lake Tahoe to the Arizona border.

The 2009 report tried everything. Cross-checked bank accounts, phone carriers, insurers, hunting licenses, gyms, parishes. Eighteen months of correlation to arrive at the phrase I now know by heart: presumably random selection.

Except.

I sit on the armrest of the couch, which the manufacturer forbids, and I do what I always do when a file resists: I look for what's too subtle to appear in a spreadsheet column.

And there's one thing. It leapt out at me in the cellar this afternoon, and no one mentions it anywhere, in any report, not a line.

The ages.

Thirty-six, thirty-seven, thirty-eight. Ten victims, and not one falls outside that three-year band. Men, women, rich, modest, believers, atheists, from the north, from the south, but all born between 1970 and 1973. A killer choosing at random from Nevada's population would hit retirees, students, a teenager. Here, nothing. The whole series fits inside a single school class.

Statistically, this is not a coincidence. It's a signature no one read.

I write in marker, under the map: "They're the same age. Why?"

The air conditioner emits a clicking sound I choose to interpret as admiration.

The next morning I commit my second mistake of the week, the first having been to open that box: I go up to the ninth with the file under my arm and hope in my tie.

Supervising Agent Brennan occupies a corner office with a view of the parking lot, which, in Las Vegas, passes for a promotion. He's a square man with a regulation mustache who calls me "Cole" the way you sigh. When I come in, he's on the phone. He waves for me to wait, hangs up, and looks at the file under my arm with the enthusiasm of a man being handed a bill.

"You're supposed to be in the basement."

I set the file on his desk. I prepared my presentation all night, so I unroll it: the NV-0834 case, the misfiled box, seal 31 vanished between the laboratory and the summary, and the age band that eighteen months of investigation never noticed. Three minutes, timed. Without a single joke. A personal record no one will ever know about.

Brennan hears me out to the end, I'll grant him that. Then he opens the file, flips through two pages, closes it.

"Herrera was convicted by a jury. The murders stopped at his arrest. The case was built by very good people, some of whom still work in this house. And you, Cole, come to tell me they all missed it, on the strength of a seal lost during a move and a coincidence of birth records?"

"Ten coincidences of birth records."

"People aged thirty-five to forty are the largest bracket in this county." He pushes the file back toward me. "Do you know what it would cost to reopen? An innocent man in prison means a real culprit outside, families having the photos pulled out again, the press, the lawyers. You don't reopen a case like this on a hunch. Especially not yours."

The "especially not yours" is a cheap shot, but I brought it in myself on a platter, with years of effort. I take back the file.

"And if I find the link between the ten?"

"You're assigned to digitization until further notice. Digitize."

In the hallway, word of my visit has outrun me, I hear it in the silence that falls as I pass the break room. Delgado, who mans the coffee machine the way others man a bar, raises his cup to me.

"So, Cole, word is we're reopening last century's files? You looking for who killed Lincoln too?"

Laughter. Three heads pop up over the cubicles so as not to miss anything.

"I've got a serious lead," I say. "A man with a hat, in a theater. But keep it to yourself, the investigation is sensitive."

That gets another laugh, half with me, half at me, the usual proportion. Delgado catches me by the shoulder as I move off, and lowers his voice, almost kind:

"No joke, drop Herrera. Rourke worked it back in the day, he still talks about it when he's had a few. That case broke careers. Yours doesn't need any help with that."

Rourke. I file the name away in a corner. A veteran from the second floor, the heavy handshake, the kind who wears his twenty-five years of service like a bandolier. If he worked NV-0834, I'll have to approach him. Later. With gloves.

The elevator takes me back down to my cellar and, to be honest, relief wins out over the sting. Down here, at least, the file waits for me without snickering.

Vance is at his counter, re-stitching the binding of a ledger with a curved needle, a craft from another time that he performs with a clockmaker's precision. He looks up as I arrive.

"Well? Does management share your interest?"

"Management has entrusted me with a mission of national scope. Digitizing. In alphabetical order, even, if I feel up to it."

He gives that little compassionate nod, tucks his needle into a felt case, and slides a yellow form toward me.

"The location search for your seal 31. I ran it last night." He taps the bottom line. "Reply from central storage this morning: the seal is on the list of items lost during the 2011 transfer. Officially destroyed by mistake along with a batch of expired archives. I'm sorry. I know it mattered to you."

Destroyed by mistake. The one piece in the whole case that held a message from the killer, an anonymous letter with a fragment of a photograph, shredded into confetti by an administrative oversight. I should have seen it coming and yet it cuts my legs out from under me. I sit on the corner of a sorting table.

"There has to be some trace left. A photo of the seal when it was opened, a description at the lab, something."

"After all this time, I doubt it." Vance folds his glasses, slips them into his breast pocket. "May I ask you a question, Agent Cole? Why this one? You have three hundred files in these aisles. More recent cases, more... approachable. Why keep at the one everyone considers finished?"

I could answer with a pirouette, I always have one in stock. But it's ten in the morning, I'm in a cellar, and he's the only one who asks the question without a smirk.

"Because everyone considers it finished. A finished case should be clean. This one isn't. It's filed away, that's not the same."

Vance looks at me for a long moment. Behind the thin lenses, his gray eyes have something attentive about them that I credit to thirty years spent listening to files rather than to people.

"Filed away rather than clean," he repeats softly. "Well put." He takes up his ledger again, his needle. "The archives thank you, Agent Cole. It's rare that anyone truly reads what sleeps here."

I spend the rest of the day digitizing, for appearances, every other scanner. The other half of the time, I copy the essentials of the ten folders into a notebook of my own, by hand. Names, dates, birthplaces when they appear in the file. If Brennan cuts off my access tomorrow, the case will fit in my pocket.

At seven, heading back up to the parking lot, I notice that of the ten birth records, seven give a place of birth, and that three of those seven list the same little town: Carver Falls, Nye County. A tiny dot on my road map, two hours to the northwest.

Three natives of the same middle-of-nowhere, in a "presumably random" series.

I stop halfway up the stairs, notebook in hand, and grin to myself like an idiot, which, at least, won't change anyone's opinion in this house.

The coincidence is hardworking. But at this point, it's putting in overtime.
