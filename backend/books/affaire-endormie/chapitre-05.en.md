# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 5: The Case Breathes

There's a moment, in certain investigations, when the file stops being an object you study and becomes a thing that watches you work. The old-timers call it the tipping point. I have no seniority, so I call it the Tuesday when everything started to disappear.

It begins small. I go down to the cellar at seven, before everyone, to copy into my notebook the birth records of victims six through ten, the ones I'm missing. Box No. 4 is in its place, aisle C, bottom shelf. The Grady folder too. But inside, the administrative bundle stops short: the birth records, the background sheet, the whole early life of Lucille Grady has vanished. What remains is the crime scene, the autopsy, the interviews. Everything about her death. Nothing about her childhood.

I check three times, on my knees on the concrete. Then I open the Kessler folder. The same surgery. Born in... the next page is missing. Novak: likewise. Someone went through my boxes with the precision of a dentist, and that someone pulled exactly the teeth I meant to examine.

I stay crouched a long while in aisle C, listening to the ventilation, and I take inventory of what it means. One: I'm not crazy, there really is something to hide in the childhoods of these ten people. It's almost good news, the most unpleasant confirmation of my career. Two: whoever did this knew what to take. Not the reports, not the photos, the origins. So they know the direction of my investigation. Three, and this is the point that dries out my mouth: my investigation exists nowhere. No report, no memo, no email. It exists in my head, in my notebook, and in what I've said aloud.

Whom have I talked to? Brennan. Delgado and the break room, by ricochet. Ellen Teller. Herrera. And Vance.

A federal office, a widow, a prisoner, an archivist.

"You're up early, Agent Cole."

Vance, precisely, emerges from aisle B, his satchel in hand, his thermos under his arm. Seven ten. I get up, dusting off my knees, and I make the obvious decision: I show him. He's the keeper of this cellar; if anyone can help me understand who came down here, it's him. And I need to see his face while he discovers the pillage.

He examines the folders one by one, his glasses pushed up on his forehead, and his face does exactly what it should: surprise, then the professional annoyance of a man whose kingdom has been desecrated.

"This is careful work," he says at last, and there's almost regret in his voice, like a craftsman before a rival's fine handiwork. "The sheets weren't torn out, look, the fasteners were reopened then closed again. Someone took their time."

"Who comes down here, Martin? Who, besides you and me?"

"Officially, anyone who signs the register." He leads me to his counter, opens the big columned ledger he keeps the old way, alongside the computer system. "Unofficially, no one ever comes. You're my first regular company in years."

His finger runs down the last pages. My name, ten times. His. Maintenance technicians in February. And then, last Friday, 4:40 p.m., a line I read twice.

Consultation of 2008-2009 files. Agent T. Rourke.

"Rourke came down Friday?"

"Late in the day. He said he was looking for a piece of evidence for an old case of his. He's a veteran, he knows the house, I didn't accompany him." Vance closes the ledger, and his gray eyes seek me out over the glasses. "Should I have?"

Rourke. The man who worked NV-0834 back then, who still talks about it when he's had a few, according to Delgado. The man whose career was built, among other things, on Herrera's conviction. If anyone in this house has everything to lose by seeing the case reopened, it's him. The reasoning assembles itself, with the ease of flat-pack furniture, and something in me, far in the back, notes precisely that it's easy. I file that reservation in a drawer. I should have pinned it to the wall.

"No, Martin. You have nothing to reproach yourself for. But do me a favor: from today, if anyone at all comes down, you accompany them. Even me. Especially me. Let the register be unimpeachable."

He tilts his head, solemn, a keeper vested with a mission. And he pours me from his thermos a coffee I do drink, that one, because at this hour you take the allies you can find.

The day could have stopped there. It chooses escalation.

At eleven, my phone buzzes. Ellen Teller. Her voice has lost the calm of the porch.

"Agent Cole. Last night, a man called me. He introduced himself as a journalist preparing an article on miscarriages of justice. He knew you'd come. He knew your name. He asked me what I'd given you."

The ninth-floor hallway begins to pitch gently.

"What did you answer?"

"Nothing. It reeked of fake through the receiver, that too-friendly tone. I hung up and wrote down the number." She dictates it to me. "Agent Cole, no one knew you'd come. I told no one. Not even my sister."

Neither did I, ma'am. That's exactly the problem. I thank her, I advise her not to answer unknown numbers anymore, and I spend the next hour doing what a sidelined agent can do discreetly: the number comes from a prepaid card, bought with cash, activated the day before, dead since. A phantom phone. Clean work. Again.

So this is where we stand, I tell myself, staring at the wall of my cubicle, which has nothing to offer me but a poster on workstation ergonomics. Someone empties my boxes, someone calls my witnesses, and that someone knows as much as I do about an investigation that doesn't exist. There are only two explanations. Either I was physically followed, to Boulder City, to Ely. Or the leak is here, within these walls, someone close enough to hear me think.

And only one person ticks the second box as neatly as the first row of my mind wants to believe: a veteran on the second floor, who built his reputation on this case, who came down to the archives Friday, and who, if Herrera is innocent, sent a man to die slowly in Ely to cover a real killer left free.

At seven p.m., I come up from the cellar by the service stairs, and I run into him.

Rourke is in the second-floor hallway, a box of files under his arm, deep in conversation with Brennan. They fall silent as I approach. Not abruptly, no, with that professional fade of people used to falling silent together. Rourke follows me with his eyes the whole way, and as I go through the stairwell door, his voice catches me, cordial as a too-firm handshake.

"Cole. Word is we've got a taste for ancient history."

I turn around. He smiles under his gray mustache, the box of files wedged against his hip, and Brennan beside him looks away with application.

"I like things well filed, Agent Rourke. Archivist's deformity."

"Then a veteran's advice, free of charge." He adjusts his box, calm. "Some cases are filed away because, from digging into them, people got very badly hurt. Filing away, sometimes, is safety. You don't move a piece of furniture that's holding up a wall."

He walks off without waiting for an answer, Brennan in his wake, and I stay alone in the stairwell with the beat of my own pulse and that sentence turning over.

You don't move a piece of furniture that's holding up a wall.

That evening, at home, I add a sheet to the living room board, apart from the ten victims. On it I write three lines in red marker.

Who emptied the folders? Who called Ellen T.? Who knew, about Ely?

And below, smaller, because part of me still resists writing it: T. Rourke, came down to the archives Friday 4:40 p.m.

I step back three paces. The wall looks at me. Somewhere in this city, the man from the third row, aisle side, already knows I'm looking for him, and he has twelve years' head start and all the keys to the house.

The case breathes now. I can hear it. The trouble with things that breathe is that they can also bite.
