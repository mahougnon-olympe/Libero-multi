# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 4: Visiting Room

Ely State Prison is a four-hour drive from Las Vegas, which leaves time to think about how you're going to justify the trip, then to give up justifying it, then to rehearse your questions mentally, then to turn on the radio and sing off-key, in posthumous solidarity with Marcus Teller.

To get in, I did the only thing within my reach: a visit request under the heading of documentary review of digitized files. It's a pretext that doesn't exist, drafted in jargon that exists all too much, and it worked precisely because no one wants to read that kind of form all the way through. Bureaucracy is a jungle, but I was born in it.

They put me in an interview room with green walls, a bolted-down table, two chairs. I wait. And when the door opens, I understand all at once, physically, what twelve years mean.

The file shows a Douglas Herrera of thirty-one, thick, solid-necked, staring straight into the lens, a man who loads trucks and fears nothing. The man who sits down across from me swims in his uniform. Hair cropped, gray. His hands laid flat on the table, motionless, hands that have learned that moving brings nothing. He doesn't ask who I am. He waits, that's all. Waiting has become his trade.

"Mr. Herrera, my name is Yaris Cole. I'm a federal agent. I work on the archives of your case."

"The archives." His voice is low, but not rusty, that of a man who speaks little but still speaks. "They send the archives now."

"They didn't send me, sir. I came alone. That's a distinction that could cost me dearly, so it might as well be good for something."

Something moves in his eyes, very fast, at once put away. Interest, perhaps, or the memory of what it was, interest.

"What do you want?"

"For you to tell me. Not what's in the file, I know it by heart. The rest."

He sizes me up for a long moment. Twelve years of empty visiting rooms, the register confirmed for me: not one visit since 2013, the year his court-appointed lawyer changed states. No family. No one. When no one comes anymore, you stop hoping for questions, and when they finally arrive, you distrust them.

"I told it a hundred times," he says. "At first. I told everyone, the guards, the chaplain, the other inmates. That I did nothing. Do you know what that gets you, here, a guy who says he did nothing? Everyone here did nothing. It's the land of the innocent, Ely. After a while, I stopped. Not because I'd changed my mind. Because it only hurt me."

"Then tell it a hundred and first time. I promise you nothing, Mr. Herrera. I'm nobody in that house, they barely lend me a scanner. But I read files in a way people dislike, and yours is the most badly filed I've ever read."

For the first time, he looks at me the way you look at someone and not something. Then he speaks.

He speaks of the van, first. The famous white van spotted near two crime scenes. He delivered for three chains at once back then, routes all over the county, seventy stops a day. His van was everywhere, that's the truth, it was everywhere because it was his job to be everywhere. The parking ticket with his print, seal 42: Reno, a Sunday, four streets from Dana Whitfield's home. He dropped pallets there for a wholesaler, every Sunday, for two years. The wholesaler confirmed it on the stand. The jury kept the ticket.

"And your alibi for Whitfield?"

"I was sleeping in the van in the wholesaler's parking lot, like every Sunday, to set off again at five. Alone." He has a joyless smile. "That's my crime, Agent Cole. I was a lone man with a van. That gets around a lot and can prove nothing. The prosecution called it an ideal cover. I called it my life."

I listen to him for an hour. I listen to him think, above all, as on the porch in Boulder City, and what I hear slowly chills me. This is not a learned account. Learned accounts are smooth, chronological, they always unfold in the same order because they were manufactured once and for all. Herrera corrects himself, hesitates over a day of the week, doubles back, gets a street name wrong and fixes it three sentences later with irritation. He remembers the way one truly remembers: badly, and honestly.

And then there's the refusal. I read it in the file and I want to hear it.

"In 2009, before the trial, the prosecution offered you a deal. Plead guilty to homicides with mitigating circumstances, in exchange for an adjustable sentence. A possibility of release after twenty years. You refused. Your lawyer begged you to accept."

"He told me: Douglas, with a jury, you get true life. Sign, and at fifty-one you can walk out." The hands, on the table, close slowly. "I answered that I would not say I killed ten people for the right to grow old outside. He didn't understand. Do you know who understands, usually? No one. The guilty sign, Agent Cole. The guilty calculate. I had nothing to calculate. I just had the truth, and the truth cost me my whole life. There it is, my proof, and it's worth nothing in a courtroom."

The silence lasts. The green room hums with fluorescent light. I still have two questions and I keep the strangest for last.

"The anonymous letters. The photo fragments some victims received before their deaths. Did anyone mention them to you, during the investigation?"

He frowns, searches far.

"Once. Early in the interrogations, one of the investigators put a photo of a piece of paper on the table, a scrap of an image, and asked me if I was the one who'd sent it. I said I didn't even know what it was. After that, never again. At the trial, it didn't come up. I thought about it, here, over time. Why did they stop mentioning it to me? If it was evidence against me, they'd have brandished it. If it was evidence for me..." He doesn't finish. He has had twelve years to finish that sentence every possible way.

Because it disappeared, Mr. Herrera. Destroyed by mistake, says the yellow form. I keep that to myself. Some truths are debts, you don't announce them until you can pay them.

"One last thing. During the trial, the hearings. Is there a detail, any at all, that stayed with you? Something no one ever asked you about?"

And there, this man who has answered everything in an even voice falls into his first real silence. His gaze leaves the table, settles on the green wall, and goes farther than the wall.

"There was a man," he says at last. "In the public seats. Not a journalist, journalists fidget, take notes, go out, come back. Him, no. Always the same seat, third row, aisle side. Every day of the trial, from the first to the last. He spoke to no one. He reacted to nothing, not to the victims' photos, not to the verdict. He watched me. The whole time." The hands slowly reopen on the table. "You'll think it's foolish. But I grew up in the country, and that look of his, I knew it. It's the look of a kid at an anthill. The kid who put the stick in and watches the ants scatter. Not cruel, not even excited. Attentive. He was looking at his work, Agent Cole. It took me years to find the words, here, at night. That man was looking at his work, and his work was me."

"Did you describe him to anyone?"

"To whom? I was the man who'd killed ten people. If I'd spoken of a spectator with a strange look, they'd have added paranoid to the file." He barely shrugs. "Nondescript. That's what he looked like. In his fifties, maybe less, glasses, gray clothes. A man made to be forgotten. I never forgot. It's my only victory in twelve years."

The guard knocks twice on the glass. Time's up. Herrera stands, and as they take him away, he half turns.

"Agent Cole. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm past that age. But if you really read files the way you say..." He searches for the word, finds it: "Read mine to the end. No one has. Even I was never allowed to read it in full."

The door closes on him.

Four hours of road on the way back, and I don't sing. I drive in the dark with, on the passenger seat, a photocopied fragment of a schoolyard, and in my ears the voice of a man who refused freedom rather than lie.

Third row, aisle side. Every day. A man made to be forgotten, come to contemplate ants.

He exists. I know it now the way you know there's someone in a dark room: nothing proves it, everything breathes it. He attended his own trial in the guise of a spectator, he watched an innocent man carry his ten deaths, and he went home at ease, somewhere in this state, where he has been aging for twelve years in the comfort of a closed case.

Sleeping, the case. Not him.
