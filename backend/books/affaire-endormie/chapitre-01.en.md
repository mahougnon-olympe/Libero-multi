# The Sleeping Case

## Volume 1

---

## Chapter 1: Box No. 7

They say the basement of the Las Vegas federal office is air-conditioned. That's a lie. What blows from the ceiling vents is the breath of a tired old fridge, and it smells of wet cardboard.

I've been down here nine days.

Officially, it's called a "support assignment for the digitization of pre-2010 files." Unofficially, the whole floor knows Special Agent Yaris Cole was shipped off to the archives because he compared the deputy prosecutor's haircut to a construction helmet, in the middle of a meeting, in front of the deputy prosecutor. In my defense, the resemblance was striking. Nobody laughed. I added that yellow suited him. Nobody laughed any harder.

That's how you end up sorting old deaths in a cellar while your colleagues hunt the living out in the sun.

"Making progress, Agent Cole?"

Martin Vance stands at the end of aisle C, a box in his arms, his head slightly tilted, as always. Vance is the civilian clerk who has ruled this basement longer than the carpet. Gray, in his fifties, thin glasses, the politeness of another century. He's the only human being to have spoken to me in nine days, if you leave out the coffee machine, and even then, it hangs up on me.

"I'm progressing, Martin. At this rate, I'll have finished digitizing 2008 by the time I retire. Will you come to the party?"

He smiles without parting his lips, sets his box on my cart and taps the lid.

"Next batch. Unsolved homicides, 2008 and 2009. Seven boxes. I've given you the first."

Then he heads off with his silent step between the shelves. Vance moves like a walking library, without noise, without haste. At first it amused me. Now I catch myself listening for the rustle of his shirt to know where he is.

I open box No. 1.

I need to explain something about myself. People take me for a clown, and it's deserved, I cultivate it. But what my colleagues call "Cole's chronic inattention" works exactly the opposite of what they think. I notice everything. That's the problem. A badly assembled file affects me the way a crooked painting in a living room does: I can smile, joke, talk about something else, and the painting keeps tilting in a corner of my skull. It tilts until I straighten it.

And this box tilts.

Not the box itself. What's inside it. A case labeled "NV-0834," ten kraft folders, one per victim. Ten homicides between March 2008 and November 2009, all across southern Nevada, a similar method, and across the first folder, a red stamp I know well: CASE CLOSED, AUGUST 14, 2009. Followed by the name of the convict. Herrera, Douglas. Life sentence.

Closed. So solved. So it has no business in the unsolved boxes.

I straighten up in my chair, and the chair protests.

"Martin? You gave me an intruder. The Herrera case, it's closed. It's got nothing to do with the orphans."

His voice reaches me from two aisles over, even, courteous.

"A filing error, no doubt. There are plenty. Just set it aside, I'll put it back."

I should set it aside.

I open the first folder.

Victim No. 1: Samuel Ortiz, 37, a heating engineer in Henderson, found on March 12, 2008. Victim No. 2: Dana Whitfield, 36, manager of a nail salon in Reno. Victim No. 3: Marcus Teller, 38, insurance broker, Boulder City. I leaf through. A pastor. A nurse. A pool salesman. Ten lives scattered over four hundred miles, ten jobs, ten neighborhoods, no overlap. The investigators of the day blackened whole pages searching for the link: no shared casino, no shared debt, no shared doctor, nothing. The final report speaks of "presumably random selection." A killer who chooses at random does exist. But ten times in a row with the same method and never a witness, that's a very hardworking coincidence.

And then Herrera. Folder eleven, the thinnest. Douglas Herrera, warehouse worker, arrested in July 2009 after the tenth murder. His van spotted near two crime scenes, a partial print on a parking ticket, no alibi. No confession either. The jury was enough.

After his arrest, not a single murder. For everyone, that sentence is as good as proof. The murders stop, so they had the right man. I reread the sentence in the summary report, underlined twice by a relieved investigator.

The painting tilts.

Because there's another reading, and it bothers me: maybe the murders stopped because the count was complete. Ten. A round number. You also stop when you're finished.

I hear myself think and I chuckle alone in the cellar. Now there's a thing. Nine days underground and I'm rewriting other people's investigations. They worked eighteen months on this, Cole. You've never closed a case in your life. They hand you the photocopier and you find a way to staple your tie.

I close the folder. I open it again.

This is the moment I should have put the box away, gone back up, offered the machine a coffee to make amends. Instead I do the only thing I really know how to do: I check the case's bookkeeping. Every criminal file has its administrative backbone, the inventory of evidence. People lie, inventories much less. It's paperwork, and paperwork is my kingdom, the whole ninth floor will confirm it with a snicker.

The NV-0834 inventory runs six pages. Seal numbers, descriptions, locations. Fibers, casts, shell casings, Herrera's parking ticket under seal 42. I go down the list line by line, the way others tell rosary beads.

Page five, I stop.

Seal 31: "Unsigned correspondence found at the home of victim No. 3 (Teller). Photographic fragment included. Sent to the laboratory on April 22, 2008."

Unsigned correspondence. Photographic fragment. I go back to the Teller folder and empty it onto the table, sheet by sheet. The scene report mentions the letter in a single line. The laboratory, nothing. No analysis result, no photograph of the seal, no return. In the final summary, eighteen months of work condensed into forty pages, seal 31 no longer appears at all. A piece of evidence enters the lab in April 2008 and the case closes over its absence, without anyone asking where it went.

An anonymous letter with a piece of a photo, sent to a man who would be murdered. And this letter evaporates between two floors of the administration.

The painting isn't tilting anymore. It's fallen off the wall.

"Have you found something interesting?"

I jump high enough that the chair remembers it. Vance is at the mouth of my aisle, his glasses in his hand, wiping them with a little gray cloth. No sound announced him, not a step, not a shirt. How long has he been there?

"The evidence inventory," I say. "Seal 31 goes to the lab in 2008 and never comes back. No report, no trace. Have you ever seen that, a piece of evidence that vanishes?"

He puts on his glasses, takes the page I hand him, scans it. Somewhere above our heads, the ventilation catches its old-fridge breath again.

"More often than you'd think," he says at last. "The labs were overwhelmed back then. Seals were misplaced during the 2011 move, too. Would you like me to run a location search?"

That's why I like Martin Vance. Nine floors of people laughing at me, and the only one who offers to help lives in a cellar.

"That would be royal, Martin."

He slips the page into his breast pocket, taps it, moves off. Then he turns back, head tilted, that thin smile that never quite reaches the eyes, but with people who live alone with boxes, smiles wear thin, I know that better than anyone.

"Don't lose yourself in this, Agent Cole. This case has swallowed better bloodhounds than you and me."

He disappears among the shelves.

I stay alone with ten kraft folders spread out in front of me, ten faces stapled to ten reports, ten lives whose common thread no one has found because, perhaps, no one looked in the right place. Nor in the right era.

I know exactly what my colleagues would say if they saw me. Cole playing detective with a twelve-year-old case, solved, tried, buried. Cole who's never finished anything and starts with other people's endings.

I take out my phone and photograph page five of the inventory.

We'll see who laughs last. And if it's them again, at least, this time, I'll have a good reason to be the office joke: I'll have tried something serious.

Above me, the ventilation chokes, coughs, starts again. Nine floors up, the sun is blazing over Nevada.

Down here, below, the sleeping case has just opened an eye.
