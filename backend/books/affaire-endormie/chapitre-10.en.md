# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 10: The List Is Complete

It takes Rourke three days to convince Brennan, and I'd give a lot to have witnessed that conversation. I know only the result: my suspension is quietly frozen, a team sets to work in a windowless room on the fourth floor, and the order comes down, formal, to rush nothing. Against a man who has been filing for twelve years, you don't search blind. You verify everything, you consolidate everything, and above all, you change nothing about the routine.

So for three days, I go down to the cellar as before. I digitize. I drink the thermos coffee. I answer "it's crawling along" to the morning question, and Martin Vance nods sympathetically, and I watch his hands take up the curved needle again, and I think: these hands cut a class photograph with a ruler, piece by piece, ten times.

On the third evening, the fourth-floor team has what it needs. The February 2009 transfer notice bears, beneath the illegible flourish, an internal registration number matching Vance's post. The fall 2009 leave coincides with two cash-paid trips to Ely, one of which a motel kept the records of: he went to see the prison where they were locking up Herrera in his place, the way you go to contemplate a delivered work. And at the back of aisle F, filed under a reference number for school records being processed, the Carver Falls registers sleep thirty meters from my digitization post, with, on page forty of the 1986-1987 enrollment register, a line in faded ink: Vancek, Martin, born 11/3/1981, admitted to first grade. Legal guardian: Vancek, Elena, laundress.

The eleventh child has a name, a line, an administrative existence. It took thirty-four years and a human filing cabinet to read it.

The warrant is signed on a Thursday at five p.m. And it's me who goes down.

It's not the custom, and Brennan objected to the end. It was Rourke who settled it, in one sentence: he'll talk to Cole, he never talked to anyone but Cole. The team locks the basement exits, the service stairs, the freight elevator. Me, I take the elevator, like every morning, with, under my shirt, the means to let the fourth floor listen in, and in my hands, nothing.

The cellar smells of wet cardboard. Under the fluorescent lights of his counter, Martin Vance re-stitches a binding, the curved needle pinched between two fingers, and he doesn't look up when the doors open.

"You're late, Agent Cole. The coffee's lukewarm."

"I know who you are, Martin."

The needle finishes its stitch. He sticks it into the felt of the case, sets down the binding, aligns it all with the edge of the counter, at a right angle, and finally looks up. Behind the thin lenses, the gray gaze is exactly the same as the first day. It's my reading that changed.

"I know," he says. "Since Carver Falls. Ada Brennerman wrote twice in 2010 to reclaim her lists. A precise woman. I always thought that if anyone one day traced it all the way back, it would be through her." He points to the chair across from the counter, the one I've sat in a hundred times. "You're recording, I suppose. Sit down, Agent Cole. You came for a story, and I file stories, it's my trade."

I sit. Above us, the ventilation catches its old-fridge breath, and Martin Vance, born Vancek, folds his hands on his counter.

"Ask your questions."

"The pump shed."

"Two days." His voice doesn't shift half a tone. "Do you know what you learn, in absolute darkness, at six years old, when you're done screaming? You learn that screaming is an expense. That crying dehydrates. That panic is a luxury of people who will be rescued. So you sit down, you conserve, and you look for something to watch. Me, I found the ants. A column coming in through the crack in the wall, working, carrying, never stopping. I watched them for two days. They taught me the only lesson that ever served me: patient work, in the dark, without a witness. The world belongs to what continues." He tilts his head slightly. "The ten who shut the door ran off to their vacations laughing. I heard the laughter through the iron. That's what I can tell you about the shed, Agent Cole. The rest belongs to the Beatty doctor, who spoke of a miracle. Doctors call a miracle what they haven't the means to call anything else."

"No one paid."

"The principal spoke of a jammed door. My mother lost the laundry, not they their vacations. We left in August, she never slept a whole night again, she died in 2006 apologizing for leaving me alone, she who had never done anything but wash their laundry." For the first time, something passes over the gray face, very brief, at once put away. "In 2007, I applied here. The criminal archives. The place where all the state's evidence comes down. You understand why, now. Before beginning a job, you prepare your workbench."

"The list."

"Ten names. I'd known them by heart since the shed, I only had to find them again, and finding people, when you know how to read a database, is a pastime." He opens the big columned ledger, his register, to a blank page, and smooths it with the flat of his hand, mechanically. "Before each one, I sent his piece of the photograph. Not a threat. A rendering. They had all forgotten, you see, that's what was remarkable. Not hidden, not repressed: forgotten. An old bit of nonsense. So I gave them back their memory, and I left them three weeks to look at it. Three weeks, Agent Cole. Two days is forty-eight hours. I left them five hundred hours with an iron door of their own. It was more generous than they were."

The recorder turns under my shirt, and I ask the question I came down for, the prosecutor's, the file's.

"You acknowledge having killed these ten people, between March 2008 and November 2009."

"I acknowledge having finished the list." He corrects me the way he'd correct a mislabeled reference number, without temper. "Ten, not one more. When the last name was struck out, I stopped, because a finished job is a finished job. Those who continue past their list are sick, Agent Cole. Me, I was a creditor. The debt settled, I filed the case away."

"And Douglas Herrera."

The silence, this time, lasts. The ventilation coughs and starts again.

"Herrera is the only entry in my bookkeeping that doesn't balance," he says at last, and it's said lower. "I didn't choose him, I didn't fabricate a single piece of evidence against him, your colleague Rourke can confirm it: the van, the ticket, everything was true and everything was worthless, it's your house that made it a guilt, not me. I could have said it with an anonymous phone call. I went to the trial every day wondering whether I'd make that call." He looks at his folded hands. "And every day, I concluded that his life sentence protected my work, and that my work was worth his life. It's the only calculation in the whole case that I'd do differently. You'll tell him. You're the only one he'll believe, you're the only one who came."

"You could have stopped me, Martin. Not handed me that box. Made the file disappear years earlier. Why keep it there, within reach?"

And there, Martin Vance smiles, the only true smile I'll ever have seen from him, thin and joyless, the smile of a man who asked himself the question before you.

"Because a work no one can read doesn't exist." He closes the columned ledger, gently. "Twelve years, Agent Cole. Twelve years sitting on the only work of my life, in a cellar where no one reads. And one morning, they send me an agent the whole building laughs at, and this agent opens a folder, and he sees the line that tilts. You wanted to know why I helped you, then framed you, then watched you come back? Do me the credit of the exact answer: I wanted to know how far you would read. No one had ever read." He takes off his glasses, folds them, sets them on the ledger, and for the first time I see his bare eyes, and they are thirty-nine years old, and six. "You read to the end. The list has been finished for a long time, Agent Cole. You can call your colleagues on the stairs, they've been making a herd's noise for a while now. I ask only a minute to close the register. One does not leave a register open."

The arrest of Martin Vance lasts four minutes and produces no noise. He holds out his wrists the way you return a book.

The rest belongs to the weeks that follow, and the weeks that follow belong to the proceedings. The judicial machine sets itself in motion for Douglas Herrera, slowly, with the same seriousness it had put into getting it wrong. I go back to Ely on a Tuesday, in the same green room, and I'm allowed only one sentence before my voice fails me, so I choose the right one: "I read it to the end." Herrera answers nothing. He lays his hands flat on the table, as always, but this time they tremble, and it's the first living thing I've seen him do.

At the office, no one apologizes to me, that's not the house's way. But one morning, the ninth-floor coffee machine serves me a cup without my having asked for anything, pushed by Delgado, who looks away as he does it, and in our dialect, that's worth decorations. Rourke retires in August; at his send-off, he raises his glass in my direction without a word, and I understand that some debts from 2008 have finally been settled, he too kept a bookkeeping.

Brennan summons me in September. I expect everything, including the permanent sidelining, and it's a form he pushes toward me: the creation of an unsolved-cases review unit, attached to the Las Vegas office. One full-time agent to start. Basement office, he specifies, laughable budget, no glory to expect from it.

"Word is you know how to read, Cole. Read."

So here I am, every morning, in the cellar with the gray aisles where an old-fridge breath blows. Another archivist keeps the counter now, a lively woman who hates coffee and keeps the register in blue pen. Around me sleep three hundred files, three hundred filed stories that are not all clean.

Yesterday, in aisle D, I opened one at random, to take the temperature.

It's already tilting.
