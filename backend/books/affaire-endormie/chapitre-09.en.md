# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 9: The Man Who Hands Out the Boxes

It's two in the morning and I haven't turned on the television, haven't eaten, haven't slept. I've been standing in front of the living room wall since I got back from Carver Falls, doing the most dizzying thing I've ever done in an investigation: I reread my own.

Not the facts. The scenes. One by one, from the start, changing a single parameter.

Box No. 7. It wasn't me who found the sleeping case. It was set on a cart for me. Next batch, Agent Cole, I've given you the first. A closed case had no business in the unsolved boxes, I said so aloud, and the courteous voice answered: a filing error, no doubt. Except that nothing, in that cellar, is filed by error. That file lived there because someone wanted it there, under his hand, in his kingdom, safe from the transfers and destructions that threaten closed cases. Hidden among the other dead, like a grave among graves. And its keeper didn't expect a human filing-cabinet named Cole to actually start reading.

Seal 31. Who suggested the location search? He did. Who reported, yellow form in hand, destruction by mistake during the 2011 transfer? He did. And who, I'll verify it first thing but I already know the answer, supervised the archive move in 2011? The move is the blessed moment of every paper graveyard, the one where losses become plausible.

The visitors' register. A columned ledger, kept the old way, alongside the system. Kept by whom? By him. The line that accused Rourke, that line that destroyed me in eight minutes in Brennan's office, it was written in a ledger of which he is the sole master, and it was he who showed it to me, with his finger, at the useful page. I didn't find the false lead. It was served to me with the thermos coffee.

The torn-out pages. Work with the fasteners reopened then closed, careful, admired by the expert himself with what resembled, I remember now, modesty.

Ellen Teller, Ely. I spoke of it to no one, and yet it was known. But my invisible investigation wasn't: it left a wake of paper. The seal 31 fragment concerned victim Teller; it was enough to know I'd photographed the inventory page to predict my route to Boulder City. And my visit request, that masterpiece of jargon I was so proud of, it was processed, stamped, archived. Everything written in that house ends up coming down to the cellar. I ran my entire secret investigation in writing, under the eyes of the one man who reads everything.

And the question of the fourth day, the one that had touched me because it was the only sincere question anyone had asked me: why this one, Agent Cole? It wasn't curiosity. It was a measurement. He was sounding the depth of the problem I represented, the way you sound a well by throwing a stone into it, and he was listening for how long the sound took to come back up.

I sit on the floor, my back to the couch, facing the wall. The class photo is pinned at the center now, in place of the road map, and the little boy in the front row looks at his invisible point on the ground, off-frame, for eternity.

Martin Vance.

Everything holds, and that's exactly the problem: everything holds too late. Because there's still a stone in my shoe, a big one, and I've been turning it over since Carver Falls. The age. The laundry woman's boy was six in 1987, so he's forty today. Vance looks fifty-five. The gray hair, the thin glasses, the ageless cardigans, that gentle slowness of an old clerk. My whole image of him rests on an estimate I never verified, because you don't verify the age of furniture.

At nine, I'm sitting in a diner on Charleston Boulevard, and across from me, before a plate he hasn't touched either, is the man I publicly accused last week.

Calling Rourke was the hardest step of my career, which, given my career, is a less spectacular sentence than it would like to be. He listened for thirty seconds on the phone and said: Hastings's diner, nine o'clock, come with everything. He came in civilian clothes, without a word about my suspension, and he has been listening to me for forty minutes, tracing circles with the dregs of his cup. Carver Falls. The photo. The pump shed. The February 2009 transfer. The rereading of my five weeks, scene by scene.

When I fall silent, he stays quiet a long moment.

"Seal 31," he says at last. "The letter with the photo fragment. You want to know why I came down to watch you in the cellar, why I've been hanging around this case since you opened the box?" He pushes his cup away. "Because that seal kept me from sleeping in 2008. It was my piece, son. I logged it myself at Teller's home. A fragment cut with a ruler, a date of '87. I said at the time we had to dig into the victims' childhoods. I was told the lab was overwhelmed, that the lead was fanciful, then that the piece was misplaced. And then we got Herrera, the van, the ticket, the prosecutor's pressure, and the misplaced piece became a forgotten piece. I signed the final report anyway." He looks me full in the face, and for the first time I see what the mustache and the medals cover. "That's my share, Cole. Everyone has a share in this case. You just arrived twelve years late with mine in your hands."

"The man at the trial," I say. "Third row, aisle side, every day. Herrera told me about him. You were there, at the hearings. Do you remember him?"

Rourke closes his eyes, searches far, and his face changes.

"The gray one," he breathes. "That's what we called him among ourselves, the guys in the back. We took him for a victim's relative, there were so many. Always alone, always the same seat." He opens his eyes. "Dear Lord. He watched the whole trial."

"He's been watching everything for twelve years. I need to see his personnel file, Agent Rourke. I'm suspended, I have access to nothing anymore. You do."

Rourke hesitates only a second, the time to weigh thirty years of regulation against one night in 2008. Then he takes out his phone.

That same afternoon, in his car, in a shopping-center parking lot, he hands me a thin folder. Civilian personnel administrative file. Vance, Martin E. Hired in April 2007 as a criminal archives technician, promoted to supervisor in 2011 after his predecessor's departure. Impeccable service record. No notable absence, except a two-week leave in the fall of 2009, after the trial closed. A vacation, in short. You rest, after a great work.

And on the first page, under the hiring photograph of a man with already-gray hair, already effaced, already ageless, the birth line.

Born November 3, 1981, in Tonopah, Nye County.

Thirty-nine years old. Forty in a few months. I spent five weeks two feet from a man my own age and I gave him fifty-five, because he wanted it so, because he manufactured an old age the way others manufacture an alibi, gray hair, gray cardigans, slow gestures, a man made to be forgotten, Herrera said, and no one is forgotten better than the old basement clerks are forgotten.

Son of: the field gives a single name. Mother. Elena Vance, née Vancek.

Ellie, from the laundry. An Eastern European name no one pronounced, planed down by America into four gray letters.

Rourke looks at the hiring photo over my shoulder, and I hear him breathe.

"April 2007," he says in a colorless voice. "He got himself hired a year before the first murder. He set up his place before he began. He sat down at the exact spot where all the evidence in the world eventually comes down, and he waited for it to be brought to him."

I close the folder. In my chest there is neither triumph nor relief, nothing but that clean cold of paintings that, at last, hang straight.

"He didn't wait for it, Agent Rourke. He filed it. For twelve years, the case slept in the arms of the one man it kept from sleeping." I look, through the windshield, toward downtown, the federal building, the cellar beneath the building. "And tomorrow morning, he'll pour me a coffee and ask me if I'm making progress."

"Then," says Rourke, "we'll have to decide what to answer him."
