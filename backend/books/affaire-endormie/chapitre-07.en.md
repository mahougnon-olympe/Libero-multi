# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 7: The Class Photo

Carver Falls isn't on the highway signs. You have to leave the 95 north of Beatty, follow twenty miles of a county road that the desert nibbles at from the edges, and the town appears all at once in the hollow of a small valley: two streets, a half-alive gas station, a rusted water tower still bearing the name in peeling letters, and the fitting carcasses of the mining works that gave birth to all this before carrying it all away.

At the census, a hundred forty-one inhabitants remain here. To the eye, on a Tuesday morning, four remain: two outside the gas station, a dog, and a woman sweeping the porch of a blue-painted wooden house, on which a handmade sign announces CARVER FALLS HISTORICAL SOCIETY, OPEN TUESDAYS.

People call it luck. I call it Tuesday, but I'll take it.

The woman's name is Ada Brennerman; she's the age of the photographs she keeps and has the eye of those who aren't done using them. When I introduce myself, without a badge, as an investigator working on old files, she looks me up and down for a second and delivers her verdict:

"You're the second in thirty years to come asking questions. The first was looking for gold. What are you looking for?"

"A school."

She goes still, the broom halfway through its stroke.

"That," she says, "is a first."

The inside of the blue house is a country in miniature: the mine, the church, the marching band, a century of Carver Falls pinned to the walls and sleeping in binders. Ada Brennerman was the postmistress here for forty years, which, in a one-street town, amounts to a ministry of intelligence. She sits me at the big table and hears me ask what I came to ask: the elementary school, the eighties, and whether by some miracle class photographs exist.

"The school closed in '93, with the rest. The building still stands, at the end of Fremont Street, if you're lenient about the word stands." She puts on her glasses, runs along a shelf of gray binders, pulls one out. "The end-of-year photos, we took them every June, the whole school in one image, we weren't numerous enough to stand on ceremony. The photographer came from Tonopah. Which year are you looking for?"

"June 1987."

She sets the binder before me, opens it two-thirds through, turns three sheets of glassine paper.

And there it is.

Eight inches by twelve, faded colors turning caramel, the original print under its sleeve. Some sixty children in rows before a wall of pale brick, the little ones sitting cross-legged in front, the middle ones on a bench, the big ones standing behind, a teacher at each end. On the left, a chain-link fence. On the ground, pale asphalt. And on the right, at the edge of the frame, a painted wooden sign: CARVER FALLS ELEMENTARY SCHOOL. In the lower right corner, in small orange figures: 06 87.

I take from my pocket the photocopy folded in four and lay it beside it, at the exact spot it was cut from. The fence continues the fence. The asphalt meets the asphalt. The three letters of the fragment, ...OOL, slot into the sign like a key into its lock.

Twelve years this fragment has awaited its image, and the image was waiting for it in a gray binder, at the Tuesday of Carver Falls.

"You've gone all pale, young man," observes Ada Brennerman. "I'll make coffee."

While she does, I work. My notebook on the left, the photo in front, and I compare, face by face, with the ten portraits I know by heart from pinning them to my wall. The file photos date from 2008, adults past thirty-five; here, they're thirteen, fourteen, fifteen. But children's faces don't lie yet, they haven't learned.

The third one standing from the left, that chin, that already-square jaw: Samuel Ortiz. The tall girl at the front of the big kids, light braids, the future salon-manager smile: Dana Whitfield. And there, in the center, a boy laughing harder than the others, head thrown back, sure of his kingdom: Marcus Teller.

I check them off. One by one. Boone. Grady. Kessler. Novak. Aldana. Pruitt. Marsh.

Ten out of ten.

They're all there, standing in the back row or nearly, the big kids of the school, the senior class of a mining town that was going to die without knowing it, frozen in the June sun of 1987. Ten children in a photograph. Ten adults in ten kraft folders. Between the two, twenty-one years, and someone, somewhere, who forgot nothing.

"What was school like, here?" I ask when Ada comes back with the coffee.

"Small. Just one, for everyone, from kindergarten to eighth grade, the miners weren't going to drive their kids to Beatty every morning. The little ones and the big ones in the same yard." She leans over the photo, and her arthritis-bent finger hovers over the rows without touching them, an archivist's habit I'm beginning to recognize. "Those, in the back, that's the class of '87, the last of the big kids before everything emptied out. The families left in clusters after, when the company announced the closure."

"Did you know them?"

"Young man, I was the postmistress. I knew their magazine subscriptions." She looks at me over her glasses. "Now, suppose you tell me which ones interest you. You've been checking off faces in your notebook for ten minutes thinking I see nothing, and you have the look of a man who drove a long way for a gray binder."

I size her up. She sizes me up. In an official investigation there would be a protocol, precautions, a framework. I have none of that anymore, and this woman is perhaps the last living memory of what happened in that yard.

So I choose the truth, for the second time this month, which constitutes a personal record no one, once again, will ever know about.

"These ten, Mrs. Brennerman." My finger follows the back row. "Between 2008 and 2009, someone killed them. All ten, one after another. A man is in prison for it, and I believe he did nothing. I believe the answer isn't in their adult lives. I believe it's here, on this photo, in this yard, in June 1987."

The silence lasts a long time. Outside, the gas station dog barks once, for maintenance. Ada Brennerman takes off her glasses, wipes them, sets them down, and when she finally speaks, her voice has changed register, it has descended somewhere older.

"June '87," she repeats. "So you already know. Or you're in the middle of knowing."

"Know what, ma'am?"

"What they did." She looks at the photo, and this time her finger comes to rest, not on the back row, but right up front, at the end of the front row of little ones, on a point I hadn't looked at because one never looks at the little ones. "And what we all let happen."

There, sitting cross-legged at the edge of the frame, half cut off by the fence, is a very young boy in a gray shirt. Round glasses too big for him. He isn't smiling. He isn't looking at the lens. His head slightly tilted, he's looking at something on the ground, off-frame, with perfect attention, as if the photographer, the whole school, and the month of June didn't exist.

Ada Brennerman puts her glasses back on.

"If you want to understand your ten dead, young man, I'm going to have to tell you about the pump shed. And about the laundry woman's youngest."
