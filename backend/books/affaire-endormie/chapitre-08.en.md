# The Sleeping Case

## Chapter 8: The Eleventh Child

Ada Brennerman tells it slowly, in the manner of people who have carried a story long enough to know its exact weight.

"The mother arrived in '86, alone with the little one. She took over the laundry behind the station, a job no one wanted. A quiet woman, kind, tired. The boy was five, six maybe. Not a child like the others, you saw it right away, and mind you I don't say that unkindly. He didn't run, he didn't shout. He observed. He could stay an hour crouched before the anthill on the vacant lot, next to the post office, without moving. People found it strange. People always find strange what is simply silent."

She turns her cup between her hands.

"At school, it went wrong from the start. The little ones ignored him. The big ones, no. You know how packs are, young man. They don't choose the weakest, they choose the most different. And those, your back row, were a pack the like of which I've rarely seen. Not cruel one by one, I suppose. Together, something else. They had made the boy their occupation. His glasses flying across the yard, his schoolbag in the water tank, games they invented just for him. They called them games. The whole school knew. The teachers knew. People said: it builds character, it'll pass with the summer. People said things like that, in '87."

"And in June?"

"In June, there was the pump shed." Her gaze crosses the window, the street, thirty-four years. "The last Friday of the school year, after the photo, precisely, the one you have before your eyes. Behind the school there was a cinder-block shed where the well's old pump slept, a windowless cube with an iron door. The big kids lured the boy in with some pretext or other, they locked him in, and they left for vacation."

She lets the sentence settle. Outside, the wind pushes something light along Fremont Street.

"He was found Sunday evening. Two days. A six-year-old child, two days in the dark, without water, in the June heat. It was old Sandoval who heard scratching as he passed by to check his traps. When they opened it..." She breaks off, chooses her words with a care that says more than anything. "He was sitting on the floor, calm, in the darkest corner. Dehydrated to the point that the Beatty doctor spoke of a miracle. And do you know what he was doing, young man? There was a column of ants coming in through a crack in the wall, a line working in the dark. He was watching it. He told old Sandoval, when they got him out, to be careful where he stepped. For the ants. Six years old, two days locked up, and his first sentence was for the ants."

The coffee cools in front of me. I think of a green visiting room, of a man who took years to find the words, at night. The look of the kid at the anthill. Herrera spoke truer than he knew. He wasn't describing an expression. He was describing a childhood memory that wasn't his own.

"What happened next?"

"What happened back then, in a town owned by a company: nothing." The word falls flat, worn with old anger. "The company had just announced the closure for '93, half the families already had one foot out the door, no one wanted a scandal on top of the shipwreck. The principal spoke of an accident, of a door that must have jammed. The big kids' parents were foremen, accountants, settled people. The boy's mother washed their laundry. There's the investigation there was, young man: the balance of power. There was never an official line. No police, no file. The mother closed the laundry in August and they left without saying where. We never heard of them again."

"His name, Mrs. Brennerman. The boy's name."

And there, for the first time since the start, the memory of Carver Falls hits a blank.

"Thirty years no one has asked me, and thirty years I've lost it." She slowly shakes her head, annoyed with herself. "The mother, everyone called her Ellie, from the laundry. A long, Eastern European name that no one here pronounced correctly, so no one pronounced it at all. The boy, people said: the laundry woman's boy. It's terrible to say, young man, but that child, we didn't call him. Even his name, we took it from him."

"There must be written records. The school registers, the pupil lists, the report cards."

"There were." She stands, goes to a writing desk in the corner, pulls out a thin strapped folder. "When the school closed in '93, all the school archives went to the county seat, at Tonopah. I helped with the sorting, I was already the memory of the place. Enrollment registers, pupil files, everything. I went back some ten years ago for an exhibition on the school, I wanted the class lists." She sets before me a sheet on Nye County letterhead. "Here's what they handed me instead."

I read. It's an archive transfer notice, dated February 2009. Subject: interdepartmental consolidation of pre-1995 school records. The registers of Carver Falls Elementary School, years 1985 to 1993, transferred on request to... and my eyes stop, go back, reread.

Criminal Archives Service, Clark County. Las Vegas.

"I found it odd," Ada Brennerman goes on. "School registers going to the criminal archives, had anyone asked my opinion, I'd have said it made no sense. I wrote twice to request a copy of the lists. They answered that the collection was being processed. That was in 2010. I process faster, personally."

February 2009. I do the math and the math chills me. February 2009: the string of murders isn't over, Herrera hasn't been arrested yet, the investigation is in full swing a hundred and twenty miles away. And while thirty investigators search for a link between ten adults, someone, somewhere, quietly sends traveling the only pages in the world where these ten adults appear together, children, on class lists. Someone buries the past while everyone digs in the present.

And he doesn't bury it just anywhere. Not a destruction, too risky, too visible. A transfer. A perfectly regular administrative document, a stamped request, a collection sent to lose itself legally in the largest paper graveyard in the state.

I know that graveyard. I spent nine days there digitizing the dead.

The Carver Falls registers sleep in my cellar. They've slept there twelve years, a few aisles perhaps from the kraft folders of NV-0834, filed by the one hand that rules those shelves, the hand that sews bindings with a curved needle, that keeps the visitors' register, that knows, better than anyone in the world, that a document doesn't need to be destroyed to disappear.

It just needs to be well filed.

The signature at the bottom of the transfer notice is an illegible flourish above a service stamp. But I no longer need to read it. I think of a sentence spoken in an aisle, under an asthmatic ventilation, by a courteous voice offering me coffee.

Filed away rather than clean. Well put.

Ada Brennerman watches me, and what she sees on my face must be enough, for she adds only one thing, very softly, as she closes the strapped folder:

"You've found him, haven't you? The laundry woman's boy."

Outside, the desert light fades over Fremont Street, over the skeleton of the school at the end of the road, over a cinder-block shed I won't go to see because some places teach nothing one doesn't already know.

"Not yet, ma'am. But I know who will introduce him to me. He serves me coffee every morning."
